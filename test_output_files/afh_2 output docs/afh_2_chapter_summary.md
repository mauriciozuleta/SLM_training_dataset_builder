## Main Concepts
- Preflight Assessment of the Aircraft: The Preflight Assessment of the Aircraft is essential to mitigate flight hazards and ensure the aircraft meets regulatory airworthiness standards and is in a safe mechanical condition. The pilot in command is responsible for determining the aircraft's fitness for safe flight, which involves inspecting its airworthiness status, following the AFM/POH, and reviewing logbooks for current maintenance, alteration, and inspection records, including required annual and 100-hour inspections.
- Visual Preflight Assessment: The Visual Preflight Assessment involves a detailed inspection starting from the cabin door and continuing through the cabin interior, checking components like carpeting, seat belts, seats, and windows for serviceability, proper function, and damage. This assessment follows the AFM/POH or a checklist, typically including checks of landing gear, switches, fuel selectors, trim, and instrument conditions.
- Outer Wing Surfaces and Tail Section: The "Outer Wing Surfaces and Tail Section" inspection requires pilots to check for deterioration, distortion, and loose or missing rivets/screws, especially along spar lines and attachment points. Additionally, leading edges should be scrutinized for impact damage, and aerodynamic devices, deicing equipment, wingtips, fairings, and non-structural covers must be inspected for proper condition and cracks.
- Fuel and Oil: The "Fuel and Oil" section details conventional aviation gasoline (AVGAS) grades, their color identification, and the critical importance of using the correct fuel grade to prevent engine damage from detonation. It also warns of the disastrous consequences of introducing jet fuel into AVGAS-burning reciprocating engines.
- Landing Gear, Tires, and Brakes: The section "Landing Gear, Tires, and Brakes" details how these components enable an airplane to maneuver on the ground and must be inspected to ensure positive control. Pilots should follow the AFM/POH for inspection, checking for fluid leaks, proper inflation, cleanliness, damage, and airworthy condition of all landing gear components and tires.
- Engine and Propeller: The "Engine and Propeller" section highlights the critical need for pilots to ensure the propulsion system functions properly prior to operation. This involves inspecting the cowling for secure fasteners, the propeller spinner for damage and alignment, and the propeller for erosion, cracks, and security to mitigate potential hazards.
- Risk and Resource Management: Risk and Resource Management in ground operations involves a pilot's assessment of risk factors and management of resources to maximize flight successes. This management is crucial as 85 percent of aviation accidents are attributed to pilot failure, requiring a conscious effort beyond basic piloting skills to reduce these failures.
- Risk Management: Risk management is a formalized process for identifying and mitigating hazards, which are conditions or events that could lead to an unplanned incident or accident. This process also involves assessing the consequences and benefits of accepted risk, with examples of hazards including marginal weather or a pilot's lack of qualification.
- Identifying the Hazard: Identifying the Hazard is the critical first step of the risk management process, as failing to recognize a hazard means its associated risks cannot be managed or mitigated. Identified hazards include marginal weather conditions that may exceed pilot skill or airplane performance, and a lack of pilot training resulting in insufficient experience for safe flight.
- Risk: Risk, as discussed in this section, is defined as the future impact of an uncontrolled or uneliminated hazard, representing future uncertainty. This can lead to outcomes such as loss of airplane control from unassessed weather or a pilot exceeding their capability due to unassessed training.
- Risk Assessment: Risk assessment determines the degree of risk for a planned activity and whether that risk is worth the outcome, allowing pilots to manage and mitigate their risk. It is a quantitative value weighted to a task, action, or event, helping pilots decide if a flight should be canceled or delayed due to unacceptable risk.
- Risk Identification: Risk Identification is crucial for preventing accidents, as pilots who fail to search for risk are unlikely to see or appreciate it. Given that even small aviation mistakes can be fatal, the use of standard procedures is of great assistance in identifying risk.
- Risk Mitigation: Risk Mitigation involves taking steps to reduce identified risks after assessment. For example, a VFR pilot facing marginal conditions can wait for improved weather, fly with an experienced pilot, delay or cancel the flight, or choose to drive.
- Resource Management: Resource Management involves familiarity with crew resource management (CRM) and single-pilot resource management (SRM) to effectively manage all available resources for a successful flight. In general aviation, SRM is more prevalent, focusing on single-pilot operations and integrating Situational Awareness, Human Resource Management, Task Management, and Aeronautical Decision-making (ADM).
- Situational Awareness: Situational awareness is the accurate perception of operational and environmental factors that affect the flight. It involves a logical analysis based upon the airplane, external support, environment, and the pilot, representing an awareness of what is happening in and around the flight.
- Human Resource Management: Human Resource Management requires pilots to effectively use all available human, equipment, and information resources to ensure flight safety. This involves effective communication with essential personnel, monitoring automated systems to maintain situational awareness, and managing information workloads to prevent overload during critical flight phases.
- Task Management: Task Management emphasizes that pilots have a limited capacity for information processing, and exceeding this capacity or experiencing distraction and fixation can lead to unattended information or the displacement of more critical tasks.
- Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM): Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM) is one of three essential skill sets for safe flight, alongside stick-and-rudder and aircraft systems operation. The ADM process addresses decision-making in the flight deck, helping pilots recognize and manage errors through steps such as identifying hazardous attitudes, developing risk assessment skills, and using all available resources.
- Ground Operations: The "Ground Operations" section describes the airport ramp as a complex and hazardous environment, emphasizing the pilot's responsibility for safe operation through proper planning and good situational awareness. Mitigating these hazards involves reviewing airport diagrams and capably managing refueling, passenger and baggage security and loading, ramp and taxi operations, and standard ramp signals.
- Engine Starting: The "Engine Starting" section outlines general hazard mitigation practices, emphasizing ensuring a clear area around the airplane, turning on anti-collision lights, and calling "CLEAR" before engaging the starter while depressing brakes. After engine start, pilots should set the engine RPM and monitor oil pressure, shutting down immediately if specified limits are not met within 30 seconds.
- Hand Propping: The section on Hand Propping details the hazardous procedure of starting an aircraft engine manually, emphasizing that it should only be performed by competent individuals following the AFM/POH due to serious risks. While most modern aircraft use electric starters, vintage airplanes may require hand propping, which necessitates a team of two properly trained people to ensure safety and proper control.
- Taxiing: Taxiing is the controlled movement of an airplane under its own power on the surface, requiring pilots to be proficient in procedures for safety. This involves maintaining situational awareness, understanding the operating environment, reviewing taxi plans, and being vigilant for obstructions.
- Before-Takeoff Check: The Before-Takeoff Check is a systematic AFM/POH procedure for verifying the engine, controls, systems, instruments, and avionics prior to flight. This check is normally performed at a suitable run-up position near the runway, where the pilot must ensure the engine reaches minimum operating temperature and divide attention between inside and outside the aircraft.
- Takeoff Checks: Before beginning the takeoff roll, the pilot should ensure runway numbers align with magnetic compass and heading indicators. As full takeoff power is applied, "Takeoff Checks" include verifying latched doors, proper control positioning for crosswind, correct and smooth engine power/rpm, and normal engine instrument readings.
- After-Landing: During the after-landing roll, the airplane should be gradually slowed to normal taxi speed with brakes while maintaining runway track and heading, avoiding significant turns at faster speeds. The after-landing checklist should generally be performed only after the airplane is brought to a complete stop beyond runway holding position markings to prevent errors, unless the manufacturer recommends specific items during rollout.
- Clear of Runway and Stopped: For the "Clear of Runway and Stopped" section, the after-landing checklist within the AFM/POH should be used due to different airplane configurations and equipment. This checklist includes items such as setting power, fuel, flaps, cowl flaps, trim, lights, and avionics for arrival airport taxi procedures.
- Parking: For parking, pilots should select a location and heading that prevents propeller or jet blast from striking the airplane unnecessarily, ideally heading into the existing or forecast wind. After stopping in the desired direction, the airplane should be allowed to roll straight ahead enough to straighten the nosewheel or tailwheel.
- Engine Shutdown: The Engine Shutdown procedure requires pilots to always use the airplane’s AFM/POH shutdown checklist for securing the airplane. Essential steps include setting the parking brake, adjusting the throttle, performing a magneto switch test, turning off avionics and the master switch, and installing chocks and security locks.
- Securing and Servicing: The "Securing and Servicing" section outlines post-flight procedures, including a walk-around inspection for general condition, oil and fuel streaks, landing gear, and obstructions. It also covers checking oil levels, adding fuel based on planned activity, and securing the aircraft by hangaring or tying down, securing flight controls, and utilizing various covers and locks.
- Chapter Summary: This Chapter Summary emphasizes determining airplane airworthiness, conducting preflight inspections, managing risk, utilizing pilot resources, and adhering to the AFM/POH and checklists. It highlights that pilots must ensure the airplane is safe and meets regulatory requirements, while also recognizing that flight safety involves proper preparation, risk management, and honest self-evaluation of their ability to act as PIC.
