## Main Concepts
- Function of Flaps: The Function of Flaps is primarily to increase the wing’s lift coefficient by changing the airfoil’s camber, which also increases both lift and drag. Flap deflection up to 15° mainly produces lift with minimal drag, while deflection beyond 15° significantly increases drag and can cause a pitching moment.
- Flap Effectiveness: Flap effectiveness depends on factors like size and type, with four basic trailing edge flap types discussed: plain (hinge), split, slotted, and Fowler. Each type has distinct characteristics regarding lift, drag, and suitability for takeoff or landing, with the Fowler flap providing the maximum lift coefficient.
- Operational Procedures: The "Operational Procedures" section highlights that pilots must refer to the Airplane Flight Manual/Pilot’s Operating Handbook (AFM/POH) for flap use, emphasizing the critical importance of pilot judgment and error avoidance during landings and takeoffs. It explains that understanding flap aerodynamics and incrementally extending flaps supports stabilized approaches, while significant flap extension creates drag that requires careful power management during the landing flare.
- Controllable-Pitch Propeller: The section on Controllable-Pitch Propellers explains that fixed-pitch propellers are only efficient within a narrow range of airspeeds. To achieve improved propeller efficiency across a wide range of operation, the propeller blade angle needs to be controllable.
- Constant-Speed Propeller: The Constant-Speed Propeller keeps the blade angle adjusted for maximum efficiency during most flight conditions, with the pilot controlling engine rpm indirectly via a propeller control connected to a governor. This system allows the engine to maintain a constant rpm and power by adjusting blade pitch in response to pilot input or changes in flight conditions.
- Takeoff, Climb, and Cruise: The section "Takeoff, Climb, and Cruise" details how a constant-speed propeller adjusts its blade pitch to optimize engine power and propeller efficiency across different flight phases. During takeoff, a low blade pitch allows maximum engine RPM and thrust, while for climb and cruise, the blade angle increases with airspeed to maintain efficiency and handle a greater mass of air at reduced engine power.
- Blade Angle Control: Blade Angle Control is managed by the propeller governor, which automatically adjusts the blade angle using oil pressure to maintain the pilot's selected rpm. Constant-speed propellers achieve pitch changes through mechanisms like oil pressure, centrifugal twisting moment, or counterweights, with the loss of governor oil pressure affecting each type differently.
- Governing Range: The Governing Range for constant-speed propellers is defined by the range of possible blade angles between high and low pitch stops. As long as the propeller blades operate within this governing range, a constant engine RPM is maintained; however, once a pitch-stop limit is reached, engine RPM will increase or decrease with changes in airspeed and propeller load.
- Constant-Speed Propeller Operation: Constant-Speed Propeller Operation involves starting the engine with the propeller control in the low pitch/high rpm position and exercising the propeller during warm-up to circulate oil and ensure system function. For power changes, increase rpm before manifold pressure and decrease manifold pressure before rpm to prevent engine stress and detonation.
- Turbocharging: Turbocharging allows aircraft to maintain sufficient cruise power at high altitudes, leading to faster true airspeeds, increased range, and fuel economy, while also offering flexibility for low-altitude flight. This is achieved by an exhaust-driven turbocharger, which uses a compressor and turbine to raise induction air pressure, regulated by a waste gate and simplified by a pressure controller.
- Ground Boosting Versus Altitude Turbocharging: The section "Ground Boosting Versus Altitude Turbocharging" differentiates between altitude turbocharging, which maintains sea level manifold pressure up to a critical altitude, and ground boosting, which uses higher manifold pressures, potentially up to 45 "Hg. While turbocharged induction air heating can cause power loss, an automatic density controller can maintain constant air density for near sea-level horsepower output.
- Operating Characteristics: The "Operating Characteristics" section details that turbocharged engine power controls require slow, smooth movements to prevent over-boosting, and manifold pressure reacts differently to RPM changes based on waste gate position. It also explains that high altitude operations can cause fuel vaporization, necessitating an auxiliary positive pressure (boost) pump to prevent vapor lock in engine-driven fuel pumps.
- Heat Management: The section on Heat Management emphasizes that turbocharged engines require careful operation and continuous monitoring of pressures and temperatures due to their higher operating temperatures and less efficient cooling at altitude. Effective heat management is crucial to prevent detrimental effects, achieved by monitoring gauges like EGT/TIT and CHT, and managing controls such as throttle, propeller rpm, mixture, and cowl flaps, with mixture being the most influential for exhaust gas/TIT.
- Turbocharger Failure: Due to the high temperatures and pressures in the turbine exhaust system, any turbocharger malfunction should be treated with extreme caution. Pilots must follow the manufacturer’s recommended procedures, or alternative procedures if the manufacturer's guidance is inadequate.
- Over-Boost Condition: The "Over-Boost Condition" occurs due to an excessive rise in manifold pressure, possibly from faulty waste gate operation. To address this, immediately retard the throttle smoothly to limit manifold pressure and operate the engine to avoid further over-boost.
- Low Manifold Pressure: Low manifold pressure, which may indicate a serious exhaust leak, requires shutting down the engine according to recommended failure procedures unless a greater emergency exists. If continued operation is warranted, use the lowest power setting demanded by the situation and land as soon as practicable, ensuring corrective maintenance is undertaken following any turbocharger malfunction.
- Retractable Landing Gear: Retractable landing gear provides increased climb performance and higher cruise airspeeds by decreasing drag. These systems, which can be hydraulic, electric, or a combination, include flight deck warning indicators and emergency operations, requiring pilots to adhere to specific operating procedures and limitations due to their complexity.
- Landing Gear Systems: The Landing Gear Systems section details two primary retraction methods: electrical and hydraulic. Electrical systems use an electrically-driven motor to raise and lower the gear via mechanical linkages, operating until limit switches are tripped. Hydraulic systems, conversely, utilize pressurized hydraulic fluid to actuate linkages, with fluid pressure regulated by pumps, relief valves, and limit switches, and can be engine-driven or electrically-powered.
- Controls and Position Indicators: The section on Controls and Position Indicators details how landing gear position is controlled by a wheel-shaped switch on the flight deck panel. Landing gear position indicators vary, often using groups of lights (green for down, amber for up, red/amber for in transit/unsafe) or integrated electronic displays, and some systems use tab-type indicators with markings or silhouettes.
- Landing Gear Safety Devices: Landing Gear Safety Devices include a gear warning horn that sounds when the airplane is configured for landing with the gear not down and locked, and mechanical downlocks to prevent accidental retraction. Additionally, safety switches (squat switches) prevent gear retraction while the airplane is on the ground, and ground locks are removable devices used to prevent gear collapse on the ground.
- Emergency Gear Extension Systems: Emergency Gear Extension Systems are designed to lower the landing gear if the main power system fails. These systems can operate via a mechanical release handle, compressed gas, or forceful extension using hydraulic fluid, compressed gas, or a manual hand crank, depending on the airplane's design.
- Operational Procedures: The "Operational Procedures" section details the crucial preflight inspection for retractable landing gear due to its complexity, beginning inside the flight deck by verifying the gear selector and position indicators. This is followed by a thorough external inspection of individual system components, including cleanliness, structural integrity, and condition of mechanisms, lines, and switches, to ensure proper and safe operation.
- Takeoff and Climb: The "Takeoff and Climb" section details the procedure for landing gear retraction after lift-off, which typically occurs when the airplane can no longer land on the runway in an emergency, considering factors like runway length, obstacle clearance, and climb characteristics. Pilots should avoid premature retraction, waiting for a positive rate of climb, and become familiar with the normal sound and feel of gear operation.
- Approach and Landing: The "Approach and Landing" section emphasizes that limiting speeds (VLE, VLO) are established for landing gear operation to prevent structural damage, and these speeds are found in the AFM/POH or on placards. It details the procedure for extending the landing gear, stressing the importance of monitoring for normal operation and confirming extension via indicators, as well as establishing a standard procedure and using a before-landing checklist to avoid unintentional gear-up landings.
- Transition Training: Transition Training to complex or high-performance airplanes should be a structured course administered by a qualified flight instructor, following a ground and flight training syllabus that is adaptable to the pilot's qualifications and the specific airplane, with the goal of achieving Airman Certification Standards.
- Chapter Summary: This Chapter Summary highlights that flying complex or high-performance airplanes requires pilots to further divide their attention during critical phases like takeoff and landing. It is essential for pilots to develop the necessary knowledge, judgment, and skills, and receive adequate training to ensure a complete understanding of the systems, their operation, and operating limitations.
