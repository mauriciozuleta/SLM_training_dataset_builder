## Main Concepts
- Use of Flaps: The "Use of Flaps" section details how landing flaps adjust lift and drag, aiding in determining the landing spot and descent angle by providing greater lift for lower speeds, increased drag for steeper descents, improved visibility, and a reduced landing roll. Flap extension causes aerodynamic changes, including pitch moments, and is best done incrementally to support a stabilized approach.
- Normal Approach and Landing: Normal Approach and Landing procedures are utilized when engine power is available, wind is light or a direct headwind, and the landing surface is firm and ample. This process is divided into five phases: the base leg, final approach, round out (flare), touchdown, and after-landing roll.
- Base Leg: The base leg is a critical judgment for pilots to set up a good landing, requiring accurate assessment of height, distance, and rate of descent for a stabilized approach. During the base leg, pilots extend landing gear, complete before-landing checks, begin descent with reduced power and a target airspeed of approximately 1.4 VSO, and apply drift correction to maintain a ground track perpendicular to the runway centerline.
- Final Approach: During the Final Approach, the pilot aligns the airplane with the runway centerline, completes the final flap setting, and adjusts pitch and power to maintain the desired rate of descent and approach airspeed. The descent angle and airspeed are continuously controlled to ensure the airplane lands in the center of the first third of the runway, with corrections made using pitch and power.
- Wrong Surface Landing Avoidance: Wrong surface landings, which involve landing on an incorrect runway, taxiway, or airport, can be caused by lack of familiarity, complacency, or fatigue. To avoid such incidents, pilots should verify the landing zone on every final approach, study airport diagrams, confirm runway alignment and number, and execute a go-around if there is any doubt.
- Stabilized Approach Concept: The Stabilized Approach Concept defines an approach where the pilot establishes and maintains a constant-angle glide path towards a predetermined aiming point on the landing runway. This approach relies on the pilot's judgment of visual clues and maintaining a constant final descent airspeed and configuration, with the aiming point appearing stationary to the pilot.
- Estimating Airplane Movement and Height: Estimating Airplane Movement and Height during short final, round out, and touchdown is critical, requiring pilots to maintain a natural head position and slowly shift visual focus between the airplane's nose and the touchdown zone while using peripheral vision. Accurate estimation depends on proper visual focus proportional to ground speed, as focusing too close or too far can lead to errors like over-control or premature ground contact.
- Round Out (Flare): The Round Out (Flare) is a slow, smooth transition from a normal approach attitude to a landing attitude, initiated 10 to 20 feet above the ground. This process involves gradually applying back-elevator pressure to increase pitch attitude and AOA, decreasing airspeed to touchdown speed while controlling lift for a gentle settlement onto the landing surface.
- Touchdown: The Touchdown is the gentle settling of the airplane onto the landing surface, normally made with the engine idling as airspeed decays to just above stalling speed. Proper landing attitude is attained by applying back-elevator pressure to ensure the main gear touches first, then holding pressure for aerodynamic braking before gradually relaxing it to allow the nose-wheel to settle.
- After-Landing Roll: The "After-Landing Roll" section emphasizes that the landing process is not complete until the airplane decelerates to taxi speed or stops, requiring pilots to maintain vigilance and positive control to prevent accidents like ground loops. Directional control and speed reduction during this phase are achieved through the use of rudder, nose-wheel steering, and brakes, with proper weight distribution on the wheels being crucial for optimum braking performance.
- Go-Arounds (Rejected Landings): Go-arounds (rejected landings) are normal maneuvers used when approach and landing parameters deviate from expectations or when it is hazardous to continue, with common reasons including ATC requirements, runway hazards, or an unstable approach. This maneuver, which should be practiced, is not inherently dangerous but becomes so when delayed unduly or executed improperly, and its proper execution involves Power, Attitude, and Configuration.
- Power: The section on Power highlights it as the pilot's primary concern during a go-around, requiring smooth, full application of takeoff power without hesitation to overcome inertia and stop descent. Carburetor heat should be turned off to obtain maximum power, as applicable.
- Attitude: The "Attitude" section explains that during a go-around, pilots must carefully manage pitch, resisting the impulse to pitch up if airspeed is insufficient and sometimes lowering the nose to gain speed. Pilots should anticipate significant forward elevator pressure and right rudder to counteract the nose-up tendency and yaw from added power, using trim to relieve control pressures and maintain a safe pitch attitude.
- Configuration: The "Configuration" section outlines the proper sequence for flap and landing gear retraction after stopping a descent or initiating a go-around. It emphasizes retracting flaps intermittently and partially before the landing gear to manage lift and drag, ensuring a positive rate of climb and safe operation.
- Ground Effect: Ground effect is a factor in every landing, takeoff, and go-around, where an apparent increase in airplane performance is due to a reduction in induced drag. This "borrowed" performance must be factored in during go-arounds close to the ground to prevent premature climbs and potential loss of altitude.
- Intentional Slips: Intentional slips are maneuvers used to dissipate altitude without increasing airspeed, adjust airplane ground track during a crosswind, or rapidly reduce airspeed when flaps are inoperative. This technique involves flying sideways through the air, which significantly increases drag and allows for a rapid descent without an increase in forward speed.
- Forward Slip to a Landing: The "Forward Slip to a Landing" maneuver is used to increase the rate of descent, typically on final approach or earlier, when the airplane is too high, often with flaps retracted and power at idle. Pilots initiate the descent by reducing power and use the slip to establish a suitable approach path, removing it during the round out for a normal landing, while avoiding common errors such as incorrect pitch adjustments or poor glidepath control.
- Crosswind Approach and Landing: The "Crosswind Approach and Landing" section addresses the common scenario where landings must be made with wind blowing at an angle to the runway, which is more challenging than crosswind takeoffs due to decreasing airspeed. It outlines two primary methods for accomplishing a crosswind approach and landing—the crab method and the wing-low (sideslip) method—recommending the wing-low method while encouraging pilots to learn both.
- Crosswind Final Approach: The "Crosswind Final Approach" section details two methods for compensating for crosswinds: the crab method and the wing-low method. The crab method involves establishing a heading toward the wind until just prior to touchdown, while the wing-low method uses rudder to align with the runway and ailerons to bank into the wind, maintaining alignment throughout the approach and landing.
- Crosswind Round Out (Flare): The Crosswind Round Out (Flare) requires continued crosswind correction to prevent drifting, similar to a normal landing. As airspeed decreases, control effectiveness lessens, necessitating increased rudder and aileron deflection to maintain the upwind wing down and avoid touching down while drifting.
- Crosswind Touchdown: For a crosswind touchdown, the crab method requires removing the crab with rudder just before touchdown to align the airplane, whereas the wing-low method maintains correction, touching down on the upwind main wheel first. As forward momentum decreases, the downwind main wheel settles, and corrective rudder pressure must be relaxed as the nose-wheel touches down to prevent swerving in airplanes with nose-wheel steering.
- Crosswind After-Landing Roll: During the "Crosswind After-Landing Roll," pilots must maintain directional control using rudder, nose-wheel steering, and aileron to prevent the upwind wing from rising. Airplanes tend to weathervane into the wind due to ground friction and greater surface area behind the main landing gear, a tendency that becomes more pronounced as forward speed decreases, making control critical to prevent issues like roll-over.
- Maximum Safe Crosswind Velocities: The section on Maximum Safe Crosswind Velocities explains that takeoffs and landings in certain crosswind conditions can be dangerous, with the FAA certifying aircraft for satisfactory controllability up to a demonstrated crosswind velocity of 0.2 VSO. Pilots must determine their aircraft's maximum crosswind component and avoid operations exceeding this capability to prevent common errors during crosswind approaches and landings.
- Turbulent Air Approach and Landing: For landing in turbulent conditions, pilots should use a power-on approach at an airspeed slightly above normal, often with partial wing flaps, to maintain positive control. Maintain adequate power throughout the approach, retarding the throttle only after main wheel contact, and after touchdown, avoid forward yoke pressure while allowing normal deceleration.
- Short-Field Approach and Landing: Short-field approaches and landings are procedures for fields with short landing areas or approaches over obstacles, requiring precise control of descent rate and airspeed to stop in the shortest possible distance. These procedures typically involve a stabilized final approach from at least 500 feet above the touchdown area, using full flaps at an appropriate point, and maintaining an airspeed of not more than 1.3 VSO.
- Soft-Field Approach and Landing: The "Soft-Field Approach and Landing" requires unique procedures for rough or soft surfaces, aiming for a smooth, slow touchdown where the wings support the airplane's weight as long as practical. This technique involves using a degree of power throughout the level-off and touchdown, flying 1 to 2 feet off the surface in ground effect, and maintaining a nose-high pitch attitude to minimize stress or prevent sinking.
- Power-Off Accuracy Approaches: Power-Off Accuracy Approaches involve gliding to a specific touchdown point with the engine idling, aiming to instill pilot judgment for accurate, power-off landings. This requires estimating gliding distance and maintaining an appropriate glide angle and airspeed, with pitch attitude controlling airspeed while power is fixed at idle.
- Emergency Approaches and Landings (Simulated): Emergency Approaches and Landings (Simulated) are training exercises designed to develop a pilot's accuracy, judgment, planning, procedures, and confidence when little or no power is available. During these simulations, pilots must maintain best glide speed, properly configure the aircraft, and plan a pattern to a selected landing area, considering variables such as altitude, wind, and landing surface.
- Late or Rapid Round Out: Starting the round out too late or too rapidly, known as "Late or Rapid Round Out," can lead to an accelerated stall, causing a hard landing and bounce, or the nose-wheel striking first. Recovery from an accelerated stall requires prompt power application, while a nose-wheel first strike necessitates an immediate go-around.
- Floating During Round Out: Floating During Round Out occurs when excessive airspeed on final approach causes the airplane to float past the desired landing point, potentially leading to insufficient runway. Pilots must smoothly adjust pitch to attain the proper landing attitude as the airplane decelerates, and if a landing cannot be made on the first third of the runway, a go-around should be executed.
- Ballooning During Round Out: Ballooning During Round Out occurs when a pilot increases pitch attitude and AOA too rapidly during landing, causing the airplane to climb instead of descend, which is dangerous as it increases height and approaches a stalled condition. If ballooning is excessive or combined with crosswinds, a go-around should be executed immediately.
- Bouncing During Touchdown: Bouncing During Touchdown occurs when an airplane contacts the ground with an improper attitude or excessive rate of sink, abruptly increasing the wing's angle of attack and lift. For slight bounces, a follow-up landing can be executed with power, but severe bounces necessitate an immediate go-around.
- Porpoising: Porpoising describes a series of nose-first jumps and dives that occur after an improperly recovered bounced landing, often caused by factors like inattention, miss-trimming, or improper airspeed control. For severe porpoising, the safest procedure is to execute an immediate go-around.
- Wheelbarrowing: Wheelbarrowing occurs when an airplane's weight concentrates on the nose-wheel during takeoff or landing, often caused by a simultaneous main and nose-wheel touchdown with excessive speed and forward elevator pressure, which can lead to a loss of directional control. Pilots can correct this by applying back-elevator pressure, maintaining the proper landing attitude, or initiating a go-around if conditions permit.
- Hard Landing: The section on "Hard Landing" explains that when an airplane contacts the ground, its vertical speed is instantly reduced to zero, and without cushioning, this impact can cause structural damage. Pneumatic tires and shock-absorbing landing gear are designed to cushion the impact, which can impose a load three or four times the airplane's actual weight depending on the severity of contact.
- Touchdown in a Drift or Crab: Touching down in a drift or crab causes the airplane to contact the ground sideways, imposing extreme side loads on the landing gear that can lead to structural failure, tire damage, or a ground loop. The wing-low method is the most effective technique to prevent drift, ensuring the airplane's longitudinal axis aligns with the runway and direction of motion throughout touchdown.
- Ground Loop: A ground loop is an uncontrolled turn during ground operation, particularly during the after-landing roll, often resulting from a pilot's failure to control an initial swerve caused by factors like drift, careless rudder use, or uneven ground. While nose-wheel type airplanes are less prone due to their center of gravity, virtually every type of airplane can be made to ground loop if mishandled.
- Wing Rising After Touchdown: The section "Wing Rising After Touchdown" explains that during a crosswind landing, the upwind wing may rise due to greater wind force and increased angle of attack, potentially causing the downwind wing to strike the ground. To correct this, pilots should immediately apply more aileron pressure toward the high wing and maintain directional control.
- Hydroplaning: Hydroplaning is a condition that occurs when an airplane lands on a runway contaminated with standing water, slush, or wet snow, seriously affecting ground controllability and braking efficiency. This phenomenon, which can be dynamic, reverted rubber, or viscous, can render an airplane partially or totally uncontrollable during the landing roll.
- Dynamic Hydroplaning: Dynamic hydroplaning is a high-speed phenomenon occurring when a water film at least one-tenth of an inch deep causes a wedge of water to lift the tire off the runway, eliminating directional control and braking. The minimum dynamic hydroplaning speed (Vp) is related to tire inflation pressure, calculated as 8.6 times the square root of the tire pressure in PSI.
- Reverted Rubber Hydroplaning: Reverted rubber hydroplaning occurs during heavy braking and a prolonged locked-wheel skid, where the heat generated causes the rubber to revert and create steam that supports the tire off the runway, requiring only a thin film of water. This insidious type of hydroplaning can persist to very slow groundspeeds and is remedied by releasing the brakes, allowing wheels to spin up, and applying moderate braking.
- Viscous Hydroplaning: Viscous hydroplaning occurs when a thin film of water, as little as one-thousandth of an inch, causes tires to roll on top of the fluid, often on smooth surfaces like rubber-coated touchdown areas, and can happen at lower speeds than dynamic hydroplaning. When suspected, pilots should use aerodynamic drag to decelerate and apply proper braking techniques, maintaining directional control with the rudder.
