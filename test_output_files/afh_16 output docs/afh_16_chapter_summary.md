## Main Concepts
- Ground Safety: Ground Safety around jet airplanes requires special caution due to the lack of a visual indicator for running engines, as jet engines pose a threat even at idle from intake suction and hot exhaust. Pilots must exercise caution during taxi and when adding power to avoid pulling damaging debris or causing damage from jet blast.
- Jet Engine Basics: Jet Engine Basics explains that a jet engine is a gas turbine operating through induction, compression, combustion, expansion, and exhaust, where compressed air is mixed with fuel, ignited, and the expanding gases drive a turbine and produce thrust. The section further details turbofan engines, which divert cooler bypass air around the engine core to produce additional thrust, improving performance at lower speeds and altitudes.
- Operating the Jet Engine: The section "Operating the Jet Engine" details how thrust is controlled in a jet engine through fuel injection, with thrust levers linked to a fuel control or computer that meters fuel flow based on factors like rpm and temperature. Pilots monitor flight deck indications for engine rotation speed, fuel flow, and gas temperatures to prevent damage from exceeding limits, noting that thrust output changes more significantly per throttle increment at higher engine speeds.
- Absence of Propeller Drag: The section "Absence of Propeller Drag" highlights that unlike piston airplanes where propellers create significant drag at idle, jet engines produce no such drag and even forward thrust. This characteristic, combined with the jet's clean airframe, makes slowing down the airplane an initial challenge for pilots transitioning to jets.
- Speed Margins: The section on "Speed Margins" explains that jet airplanes define maximum operating speeds as VMO (knots) and MMO (Mach number), which pilots monitor using combined airspeed and Mach indicators, often with a "barber pole" display. Exceeding these limits can lead to significant handling changes, shock waves, buffeting, and potential loss of control, especially beyond the critical Mach number (MCR) where airflow over the wing first equals Mach 1.0.
- Mach Buffet: Mach buffet arises when airflow separates on a wing's upper surface behind a shock wave, caused by either a strong shock at high-speed cruise or airflow exceeding Mach 1 over the surface at low-speed cruise. This phenomenon defines the "coffin corner," an altitude where an airplane is at the limits of both high-speed and low-speed Mach buffet, requiring pilots to understand its causes for appropriate response and to select cruising parameters with sufficient margin.
- Low-Speed Flight: Low-Speed Flight in jet airplanes is challenging due to wings designed for high-speed flight, resulting in less area, lower aspect ratio, and thin airfoil shapes. Unlike piston aircraft, jet airplanes exhibit a lack of speed stability below VMD, where a decrease in speed can lead to increased drag, a speed divergence, and a serious sink rate, which is a critical aspect of jet-airplane flying.
- Stalls: Stalls in swept-wing jet airplanes differ from straight-wing aircraft, characterized by a more gradual lift buildup and less dramatic lift loss, but with a natural tendency for tip stalls and potential for high drag and descent rates. Some T-tail configurations are also susceptible to deep stalls, where the tail loses effectiveness, though these are avoidable by observing limitations and utilizing warning systems.
- Drag Devices: Drag devices, such as spoilers and speed brakes, are equipped on jet airplanes to reduce speed or increase descent rate due to their low drag design. Spoilers primarily spoil lift and increase drag, while speed brakes are designed to produce drag, both aiding in controlling the aircraft's performance.
- Thrust Reversers: Thrust reversers assist in decelerating jet airplanes during landing by redirecting exhaust gases, supplementing wheel brakes. These systems, typically target or cascade reversers, are more effective at high speeds and require pilots to be aware of potential nose-up pitch effects upon selection.
- Pilot Sensations in Jet Flying: In "Pilot Sensations in Jet Flying," pilots transitioning to jets experience response differences, increased control sensitivity, and a faster flight tempo, alongside slower airspeed changes due to factors like long spool-up times and clean aerodynamics. They must also learn to manage pitching tendencies from power changes and drag device deployment, using trim effectively and relying more on instruments at higher pitch attitudes.
- Jet Airplane Takeoff and Climb: This section on Jet Airplane Takeoff and Climb provides generic information, assuming a two-pilot crew for most civilian jet airplanes. It emphasizes that FAA-approved AFM procedures or specific air operator/training center procedures take precedence over this generic information if conflicts arise.
- V-Speeds: The V-Speeds section defines various critical speeds that affect a jet airplane’s takeoff performance and safety. These speeds, such as V1 (takeoff decision speed), VR (rotation speed), and V2 (takeoff safety speed), must be understood by pilots for effective takeoff planning.
- Takeoff Roll: The Takeoff Roll involves aligning the airplane in the runway center, setting power according to AFM specifications, and checking engine instruments before brake release. Pilots must compute and review takeoff data, and the pilot flying concentrates on directional control during the takeoff roll.
- Rejected Takeoff: The "Rejected Takeoff" section details various reasons for a rejected takeoff (RTO), including engine failure or runway abnormalities, and notes that ill-advised decisions and improper pilot technique contribute to most takeoff-related commercial accidents. It emphasizes that the go/no-go decision should be made before V1, with deceleration beginning no later than V1, as stopping performance can be degraded by numerous factors.
- Rotation and Lift-Off: The section "Rotation and Lift-Off" emphasizes that rotation and lift-off in a jet airplane require planning, precision, and a fine control touch, with the objective to initiate rotation at VR to achieve VLOF and V2 speed at 35 feet AGL. Proper rotation at the correct speed and rate to the right attitude ensures the airplane leaves the ground at the right speed and within the right distance, as early or late rotation can negatively impact takeoff performance and safety.
- Initial Climb: The "Initial Climb" section outlines procedures for maintaining pitch attitude and takeoff power, retracting landing gear after a positive rate of climb, and delaying flap retraction until appropriate altitudes. It also emphasizes monitoring flight parameters, managing power reductions, adhering to speed limits, and understanding climb techniques like the zoom climb.
- Jet Airplane Descent and Approach: The ideal jet airplane descent and approach involves reducing power to flight idle and slowing to L/DMAX, followed by a sequence of deceleration, configuration, and final approach steps. However, traffic and time considerations typically lead to a deviation from this ideal, resulting in a descent profile with three segments and two speed reductions.
- Descent Planning: Descent Planning for jet-powered airplanes involves determining the top of descent (TOD) based on altitude, adjusted for wind, typically approximating a 3-degree path. A common estimate for TOD is calculated by multiplying the planned descent (in thousands of feet) by three and adding distance for speed reductions, with variables such as wind, field elevation, and ATC vectors affecting the final calculation.
- Descent Energy Management: Descent Energy Management involves pilots periodically checking progress against altitude/distance targets and adjusting the aircraft's energy state. Pilots primarily manage descent by adjusting speed, which significantly impacts the rate and angle of descent, or by using power and speed brakes to correct high or low energy states and maintain the desired flight path.
- Jet Engine Landing: The "Jet Engine Landing" section details that 14 CFR part 25, section 25.125 defines the horizontal distance required to land a jet airplane from 50 feet above the surface to a complete stop. Pilots determine actual landing requirements using FAA-approved data, applying a 40% safety cushion for dry runways (dividing usable length by 1.67) and an additional 15% for wet runways (dividing by 1.92).
- Landing Speeds: The "Landing Speeds" section details specific speeds crucial for landing a jet airplane, including VSO (stall speed in landing configuration), VREF (1.3 times VSO), Approach climb, and Landing climb speeds. These speeds, which should be calculated for every landing and posted, guide pilots through traffic pattern maneuvering and the final approach, ensuring adequate performance for go-arounds.
- Significant Differences: The section on Significant Differences explains that jet airplanes are less forgiving than propeller-powered airplanes during approaches and when correcting errors, requiring more stable and deliberate flight conditions. This is primarily due to the absence of propeller slipstream for immediate lift, slower engine response at low RPM, and higher approach speeds that demand careful planning.
- Stabilized Approach: The Stabilized Approach is crucial for meeting performance chart assumptions, which include an exact 50-foot threshold height at 1.3 times VSO and touchdown in a target zone. Key elements involve being in the landing configuration by 1,000 feet AGL, on profile with an optimum glidepath, and having indicated airspeed within 0-10 knots of target by 500 feet AGL, at which point a go-around should be initiated if the approach is not stabilized.
- Approach Speed: Approach Speed control is critical on final approach, requiring immediate detection and correction of speed deviations to prevent high sink rates or exceeding VREF. Excess approach speed significantly increases stopping distance and the likelihood of an extended flare, emphasizing the importance of proper speed management and anticipatory adjustments.
- Glidepath Control: Glidepath Control emphasizes an optimum 3° glidepath angle, warning that flat approaches or excessive height over the threshold significantly increase landing distance. Pilots should aim to arrive at the approach threshold window exactly 50 feet above the runway to avoid these common errors.
- The Flare: The Flare reduces the approach rate of descent for touchdown in jet airplanes, which should be flown onto the runway rather than held off to avoid significantly increasing landing distance. This maneuver is initiated by increasing pitch attitude to reduce the sink rate to 100–200 fpm when the landing gear is approximately 15 feet above the runway, while smoothly reducing thrust to idle.
- Touchdown and Rollout: The "Touchdown and Rollout" section details that after main wheel contact, pilots must maintain directional control and initiate stopping, lowering the nose-wheel promptly to improve deceleration. Stopping forces include wheel braking, reverse thrust, and aerodynamic braking, with brakes being most effective at low speeds and reverse thrust/drag at high speeds.
- Jet Airplane Systems and Maintenance: Jet Airplane Systems and Maintenance are governed by 14 CFR part 25 for certification, ensuring high performance and safety margins that are realized through strict adherence to the FAA-approved AFM. Furthermore, 14 CFR part 91.213(a) requires an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL) for turbine-powered airplanes to operate with inoperable equipment under specified conditions.
- Minimum Equipment List: The Minimum Equipment List (MEL) serves as a reference guide for dispatchers and pilots to determine if takeoff with inoperative instruments or equipment is authorized under regulatory requirements. It allows for the deferral of repairs for items on approved aircraft systems and components, but if particular items do not allow for safe operation, they do not appear on the MEL and takeoff is not authorized until repaired.
- Configuration Deviation List: A Configuration Deviation List (CDL) is used similarly to an MEL, but it specifically addresses missing external aircraft parts rather than failing internal systems. These typically include elements such as service doors, landing gear doors, and static dischargers.

It also notes that higher speeds require smaller control inputs, descents need more planning, and provides resources for further information on turbojet operations and upset recovery.
