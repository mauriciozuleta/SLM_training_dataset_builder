## Main Concepts
- General: The "General" section highlights that multiengine airplanes operate differently during an engine failure, where the loss of thrust significantly impacts both performance and control. This includes a substantial reduction in climb performance and asymmetrical thrust creating control issues, both crucial factors for safe One Engine Inoperative (OEI) flight.
- Terms and Definitions: The "Terms and Definitions" section reviews familiar V-speeds and introduces several new V-speeds unique to twin-engine airplanes, particularly those related to One Engine Inoperative (OEI) operation. These include speeds like VXSE, VYSE, VSSE, and VMC, which are crucial for understanding multiengine aircraft performance and control.
- Operation of Systems: This "Operation of Systems" section discusses systems and equipment generally found in multiengine airplanes. It notes that while multiengine airplanes share many features with complex single-engine airplanes, certain features are more often found in aircraft with two or more engines.
- Feathering Propellers: Feathering Propellers allows pilots of multiengine airplanes to streamline the propeller blades of an inoperative engine with the relative wind, minimizing propeller drag and stopping engine rotation. This feature is crucial as it permits continued flight after an engine failure, whereas an unfeathered, windmilling propeller at small blade angles can create significant drag, making the aircraft difficult to control.
- Propeller Synchronization: Propeller Synchronization systems, including synchronizers and synchrophasers, are installed in multiengine airplanes to eliminate propeller noise and vibration by precisely matching propeller RPMs and, for synchrophasers, adjusting blade positions. Pilots engage these systems after coarsely matching RPMs, but they must be disengaged for critical phases of flight such as takeoff, landing, and single-engine operation.
- Fuel Crossfeed: Fuel crossfeed systems, unique to multiengine airplanes, allow an engine to draw fuel from a fuel tank in the opposite wing, primarily serving as an emergency procedure to extend range and endurance during one-engine-inoperative flight. Pilots must follow specific AFM/POH procedures for functional checks and should never use crossfeed during takeoff or normal landing operations with both engines operating.
- Combustion Heater: Combustion heaters, found on multiengine airplanes, are small furnaces that burn gasoline to provide heated air for occupant comfort and windshield defogging. These units are thermostatically operated, include an hour meter, and feature over-temperature protection via a thermal switch that requires a cool-down period to prevent it from tripping.
- Flight Director/Autopilot: The Flight Director/Autopilot (FD/AP) system integrates various flight signals to display computed commands on a Flight Command Indicator (FCI), which replaces the conventional attitude indicator. This system can operate off, as a flight director where the pilot flies to displayed commands, or with the autopilot engaged to automatically maneuver the airplane.
- Yaw Damper: The Yaw Damper is a servo that moves the rudder in response to detected yaw rate or lateral Gs, primarily reducing motion about the vertical axis caused by turbulence and providing a smoother ride. It should be off for takeoff and landing, may have restrictions with one engine inoperative, and can be engaged independently of the autopilot.
- Alternator/Generator: The Alternator/Generator section describes how each engine on a multiengine aircraft has an alternator or generator, with paralleling circuitry to ensure the electrical system load is shared equally. In the event of a failure, the inoperative unit can be isolated, allowing the remaining unit to power the entire electrical system, potentially requiring load shedding.
- Nose Baggage Compartment: Nose baggage compartments are common on multiengine airplanes, and pilots must ensure their latches and locks are properly secured during preflight to prevent the door from opening or contents from shifting, which can distract from flying the airplane. Additionally, the compartment interior should be inspected to verify its contents are secured and within limits.
- Anti-Icing/Deicing Equipment: The section on Anti-Icing/Deicing Equipment details systems installed on multiengine airplanes, classifying them as anti-icing to prevent ice formation or deicing to remove accumulated ice from protected surfaces. Pilots must consult the AFM/POH for specific approvals and limitations, as the presence of this equipment does not automatically approve flight in icing conditions.
- Performance and Limitations: The "Performance and Limitations" section defines critical terms for multiengine airplane operations, such as accelerate-stop distance, accelerate-go distance, climb gradient, all-engine service ceiling, and single-engine service ceiling. Understanding these performance capabilities and limitations is essential for pilots to make informed takeoff decisions and plan for potential engine failures.
- Weight and Balance: The section on Weight and Balance explains that while the concept is similar to single-engine airplanes, its execution is more complex in multiengine aircraft due to numerous loading areas and varied interior configurations. It details the different terminologies for calculating empty weight, such as GAMA's "basic empty weight" and older formats' "licensed empty weight," highlighting the inclusion or exclusion of oil as a key distinction between them.
- Ground Operation: For ground operation, multiengine airplanes require greater vigilance due to increased wingspan and are less nimble than single-engine planes, though differential power aids turning by minimizing brake use and turning radius. Pilots should avoid pivoting on an inboard wheel with brakes and differential power, keep cowl flaps fully open unless otherwise directed, and defer strobe lights until taxiing onto the active runway.
- Normal and Crosswind Takeoff and Climb: For Normal and Crosswind Takeoff and Climb, pilots must complete pre-takeoff checks, ensure traffic clearance, and position flight controls for any crosswind. Takeoff power, often full throttle, should be set according to the AFM/POH, with smooth throttle application for turbocharged engines, and brakes may be held until significant power is established.
- Short-Field Takeoff and Climb: The Short-Field Takeoff and Climb differs from normal takeoffs in airspeeds and initial climb profile, requiring adherence to the AFM/POH and specific techniques. This involves accelerating to VX for initial obstacle clearance before transitioning to VY, while also considering the critical implications of engine failure at low airspeeds and the proximity of VX/VXSE to VMC, especially with flaps.
- Rejected Takeoff: The "Rejected Takeoff" section explains that a multiengine takeoff can be rejected for the same reasons as a single-engine airplane. Upon rejection, pilots should promptly close both throttles and maintain directional control with rudder, nose-wheel steering, and brakes, prioritizing control during deceleration over stopping in the shortest possible distance.
- Level Off and Cruise: For "Level Off and Cruise," pilots should allow the airplane to accelerate to cruising airspeed at climb power before setting cruise power and RPM, following manufacturer tables for optimal performance. Fuel management in multiengine airplanes is complex, often requiring selection between tanks or fuel transfer, with varying electric fuel pump operations and specific limitations, emphasizing the need for thorough AFM/POH knowledge.
- Slow Flight: Slow flight in a multiengine airplane involves maneuvering in various configurations and flight attitudes, with no unusual characteristics. It must be conducted at a safe altitude (no lower than 3,000 feet AGL), requires monitoring temperatures, and prohibits simulated engine failures or disabling safety devices due to proximity to VMC and VSSE.
- Spin Awareness and Stalls: Regarding Spin Awareness and Stalls, multiengine airplanes are not approved for spins and generally have very poor spin recovery characteristics. Therefore, it is prudent to practice spin avoidance and maintain high awareness of situations that can result in an inadvertent spin.
- Spin Awareness: Spin awareness is critical in multiengine airplanes because a spin requires a stalled condition and a yawing moment, which can be generated by rudder input or asymmetrical thrust. This awareness is paramount during VMC demonstrations, stall practice, slow flight, or any condition of high asymmetrical thrust at low speed/high AOA, with instructors needing to monitor airspeed and bank to prevent inadvertent spins.
- Stall Training: Stall Training for multiengine airplanes recommends practicing stalls at an altitude allowing recovery no lower than 3,000 feet AGL. The most important recovery step is to reduce the angle of attack, and pilots should avoid single-engine stalls or simulated engine failures during stall entry and recovery due to the likelihood of a departure from controlled flight.
- Power-Off Approach to Stall (Approach and Landing): The Power-Off Approach to Stall (Approach and Landing) maneuver simulates problematic approach and landing scenarios, initiated by clearing the area, configuring for landing, establishing a stabilized descent, and smoothly increasing the AOA to induce a stall warning. Recovery involves reducing the AOA until the warning is eliminated, rolling wings level with coordinated rudder, applying power, and accelerating to VX or VY while adjusting trim and flap settings.
- Power-On Approach to Stall (Takeoff and Departure): The Power-On Approach to Stall (Takeoff and Departure) simulates problematic takeoff scenarios, initiated by configuring the airplane for takeoff, slowing to lift-off speed, and increasing power while increasing AOA to induce a stall warning. Recovery involves reducing AOA to eliminate the warning, rolling wings level with coordinated rudder, applying power as needed, retracting gear and flaps, and accelerating to VX or VY.
- Full Stall: Practicing a Full Stall is recommended only with a qualified flight instructor in a structured lesson, whether power-off or power-on. The training aims to provide pilots experience with handling characteristics and dynamic cues near and at full stall, while reinforcing proper stall recovery procedures, emphasizing the primary step of lowering the angle of attack first.
- Accelerated Approach to Stall: The "Accelerated Approach to Stall" maneuver is performed with an approximate 45° bank, not exceeding the manufacturer's recommended airspeed, VA, or VO, and should be completed no lower than 3,000 feet AGL. Pilots initiate the maneuver by increasing back pressure in a coordinated 45° turn while reducing speed, and upon stall warning, recover by reducing the AOA, rolling wings level, and adding power.
- Normal Approach and Landing: The "Normal Approach and Landing" in multiengine airplanes necessitates advance planning for descent due to higher cruising speeds, emphasizing engine parameter maintenance and early checklist initiation for the traffic pattern. The FAA recommends a stabilized approach concept, where the airplane is on speed, in trim, configured, and tracking the centerline within 500 feet AGL on final approach.
- Crosswind Approach and Landing: The "Crosswind Approach and Landing" section details that multiengine airplanes, while often easier to land in a crosswind, utilize a combination of crab and wing-low methods to align the longitudinal axis with the runway centerline before touchdown, with the upwind wing touching down first. It also highlights that some aircraft have AFM/POH limitations against slips to prevent fuel starvation and that differential power can assist, though rudder and aileron are generally preferred for quicker reactions to changing wind conditions.
- Short-Field Approach and Landing: The "Short-Field Approach and Landing" emphasizes full flaps, a stabilized approach with a constant angle of descent, and precise airspeed control. After touchdown, maximum stopping effort is achieved by retracting wing flaps, adding back pressure, and applying heavy braking, though flap retraction on rollout should only be considered when operationally necessary due to associated risks.
- Go-Around: A go-around involves advancing throttles to takeoff power and adjusting pitch to arrest the sink rate, then establishing a climb attitude with a target airspeed of VY or VX. Subsequently, flaps are retracted from full to an intermediate position, followed by landing gear retraction upon achieving a positive rate of climb, and finally, the remaining flaps are retracted.
- Engine Inoperative Flight Principles: Engine Inoperative Flight Principles focus on two main considerations: performance and control. Pilots learn to operate for maximum rate of climb performance at the blue radial indicated airspeed without sideslip, and to recognize and recover from loss of directional control associated with the red radial indicated airspeed by performing a VMC demonstration, during which sideslip occurs.
- Derivation of VMC: The section "Derivation of VMC" explains that VMC is a manufacturer-established speed, published in the AFM/POH, but it is not a fixed airspeed under all conditions. During aircraft certification, VMC is determined under both dynamic and static conditions, with the higher of the two published, considering the critical engine's failure.
- VMC Demo: The VMC Demo in flight training involves performing the maneuver at least 3,000 feet AGL with specific aircraft configurations, simulating an engine failure (e.g., left engine idle, right engine takeoff power) while slowly decelerating. The pilot counteracts the asymmetrical thrust with rudder and bank until an uncontrollable yaw occurs, at which point recovery is initiated by retarding the operating engine's throttle and lowering pitch to regain speed.
- VMC Demo Stall Avoidance: The "VMC Demo Stall Avoidance" section highlights that VMC decreases with altitude while stalling speed (VS) remains constant, and a stall under asymmetrical power can lead to a dangerous spin entry. Therefore, during a VMC demonstration, pilots should immediately terminate the maneuver at the first symptoms of an impending stall to avoid a stalled condition with asymmetrical thrust.
- OEI Climb Performance: For best OEI climb performance, pilots must achieve minimum drag at VYSE with maximum available power, primarily by minimizing sideslip after retracting flaps and gear and feathering the propeller. In OEI flight, zero sideslip is not indicated by a centered ball but by a specific combination of rudder and bank, which aligns with AFM/POH performance charts.
- Low Altitude Engine Failure Scenarios: This section on Low Altitude Engine Failure Scenarios emphasizes that engine failure during initial climb after takeoff is critical, posing risks of losing directional control, performance, and flying speed. Pilots must have an emergency plan, maintain airplane control, and follow manufacturer procedures due to minimal altitude and time, and the significant drag and yaw from a windmilling propeller.
- Landing Gear Down: This section, "Landing Gear Down," outlines procedures for engine failure when the landing gear is still down or when single-engine climb performance is inadequate after takeoff. In these situations, pilots should close throttles, maintain airspeed, descend for a normal landing, or land on whatever lies ahead, prioritizing a controlled landing to avoid attempting flight beyond the airplane's performance capability.
- Control: The "Control" section highlights that maintaining control, particularly directional control with prompt rudder application to stop yaw, is the primary consideration following engine failure during takeoff. It is critical to keep airspeed above VMC and, after rudder, use a slight bank toward the operative engine to minimize drag and maximize performance.
- Configuration: The Configuration section outlines the prompt execution of memory items from the engine failure after takeoff checklist to configure the airplane for climb. This involves assuming VYSE, setting takeoff power, retracting flaps and landing gear, and identifying, verifying, and feathering the failed engine, with identification primarily through control inputs.
- Climb: The "Climb" section explains that after establishing directional control and configuring the airplane, the bank angle should be reduced for best climb performance, suggesting a 2° bank with a slight slip towards the operative engine for zero sideslip. VYSE is maintained with pitch control, and climbs should be made straight ahead or with shallow turns to at least 400 feet AGL before attempting a return to the airport, as turning flight reduces climb performance.
- Checklist: The "Checklist" section details that after completing memory items for an engine failure after takeoff, pilots should deliberately review and accomplish printed checklists, prioritizing airplane control and avoiding haste to prevent errors. It also explains that memory items confirm conditions or initiate actions across various scenarios, and introduces memory aids like "dead foot—dead engine" for identifying a failed engine.
- Engine Failure During Flight: This section on Engine Failure During Flight explains that failures well above the ground allow for better airplane control and diagnosis compared to those at lower speeds and altitudes, emphasizing that maintaining airplane control is paramount. It details that not all power losses are catastrophic, with many being related to issues like fuel starvation that may be remedied by actions such as switching fuel tanks, adjusting mixture, or using carburetor heat.
- Engine Inoperative Approach and Landing: The "Engine Inoperative Approach and Landing" is essentially the same as a two-engine approach, but with reduced and asymmetrical thrust requiring a higher power setting on the operative engine. Pilots must maintain VYSE until the landing is assured, manage flap extension based on performance, and be prepared for a rudder trim change and increased float during the round out.
- Multiengine Training Considerations: The section on "Multiengine Training Considerations" outlines that safe multiengine flight training necessitates thorough preflight briefings, a clear understanding of how simulated emergencies will be introduced, and the essential use of checklists. It emphasizes that surprising learners with emergencies is hazardous and many procedures can be safely practiced on the ground without engines running.
- Chapter Summary: This Chapter Summary emphasizes that while small multiengine airplanes handle like single-engine aircraft with both engines functioning, a competent pilot must acquire additional knowledge and skills for an engine loss. The chapter detailed loss of directional control (VMC) and best performance with an inoperative engine (VYSE), highlighting the importance of understanding these concepts to mitigate risks and ensure safe multiengine operations.
