- Sport Pilot Certificate: The Sport Pilot Certificate, created in 2004, established lower minimum training time requirements for pilots wishing to operate only Light Sport Airplanes (LSA). Pilots holding higher-level certificates can also fly LSAs with appropriate ratings and a valid U.S. driver's license, unless their most recent medical certificate was denied, revoked, suspended, or withdrawn, in which case an FAA medical certificate is required.

S-LSA owners must comply with manufacturer safety directives and any FAA Airworthiness Directives pertaining to FAA-certified components installed on the LSA.

Pilots transitioning to LSAs must understand the specific inspection concerns for each construction type, including fabric condition, aluminum corrosion, or composite cracks and delamination.
- Engines: Engines in Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs) vary significantly, ranging from FAA-certificated to non-FAA-certificated designs, which presents new training opportunities and challenges for transitioning pilots. Most LSA engines are water-cooled, 4-cycle, and carbureted, operating at much higher RPMs that necessitate a gearbox to reduce propeller RPM, resulting in different vibration and noise signatures.
- Instrumentation: The Instrumentation section explains that Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs) often feature advanced flight and engine instrumentation, including Electronic Flight Instrumentation Systems (EFIS). EFIS provides comprehensive flight and engine data on liquid crystal displays, offering a cost-effective, low-maintenance alternative to traditional mechanical instruments, but requiring proper pilot training to avoid loss of situation awareness.
- Weather Considerations: Weather Considerations are particularly significant for Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs) due to their lighter weight, making them more susceptible to strong winds, turbulence, and convective weather. LSA pilots should carefully manage weather-related risks by considering hazardous conditions, using personal minimums, and maintaining greater distances from convection, as well as through careful planning and understanding of the flying environment.

While most skills are transferable, pilots should seek competent flight instruction and consult the POH due to significant variations in LSA performance, equipment, systems, and construction.

Pilots should allow adequate time to become familiar with the LSA by reviewing the POH with a flight instructor, covering its limitations, systems, performance, weight and balance, normal and emergency procedures, and handling requirements.

Pilots must become familiar with these controls, perform full range of motion checks, and understand the often electrically actuated elevator trim systems before takeoff.

Pilots must also examine water-cooled engine systems for condition and leaks, and verify the smooth operation of split flaps, which may require a mirror for proper inspection.

Engine starting procedures for LSAs range from simple for newer models to normal for standard certificated engines, and pilots must practice unique canopy or door latching mechanisms to prevent in-flight emergencies.
- Taxi: When taxiing Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs), pilots may encounter full-castoring or steerable nose-wheels, or conventional tailwheel gear. Full-castoring nose-wheels require differential brakes and practice to maintain centerline, while tailwheel LSAs should initially be taxied in no-wind conditions to develop proper flight control responses due to their light weight.
- Takeoff and Climb: The "Takeoff and Climb" performance of Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs) is spirited due to their high horsepower-to-weight ratio, leading to low rotation and climb speeds with impressive rates. Pilots should fly published speeds, manage light stick forces to avoid over-control, and actively clear airspace during climbs due to potential visibility issues, ensuring the airplane is properly trimmed.
- Cruise: The "Cruise" section outlines the process for establishing cruise flight in Light Sport Airplanes (LSAs), including accelerating to speed, reducing power, adjusting pitch, and trimming, while noting that pilots should use light fingertip pressures for control due to potentially light stick forces. It also covers practicing typical LSA maneuvers like steep turns, slow flight, and stalls, which may feel simpler due to light controls but still adhere to basic aerodynamics.
- Approach and Landing: The "Approach and Landing" in an LSA is routine, with pattern speeds around 60 knots and lower flap limit airspeeds requiring careful management. Due to light control forces and weight limits, crosswind landings are critical, necessitating smooth control application and personal minimums to avoid over-controlling or exceeding the aircraft's capabilities.

Pilots must be prepared to execute immediate action items from memory and quickly locate procedures for issues like trim runaway or EFIS failure, which an effective training program covers.
