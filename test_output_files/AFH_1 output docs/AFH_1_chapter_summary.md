## Main Concepts
- Role of the FAA: The "Role of the FAA" section details how the Federal Aviation Administration promotes aviation safety by prescribing safety standards for civil aviation through the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), specifically 14 CFR. This includes establishing standards for airmen and aircraft certification, outlining operating rules, and issuing Airworthiness Directives (ADs) when unsafe conditions exist.
- Flight Standards Service: The FAA’s Flight Standards Service (FS) sets aviation standards for airmen and aircraft operations in the United States and globally. The local Flight Standards District Office (FSDO) serves as the primary interface, responsible for certification, surveillance, accident investigation, and administering FAA practical tests for pilots and flight instructors.
- Role of the Pilot Examiner: The "Role of the Pilot Examiner" section describes Designated Pilot Examiners (DPEs) as private citizens designated by the FAA to administer practical tests for pilot certificates and ratings, acting as representatives of the FAA Administrator. DPEs perform specific, limited pilot certification tasks under FSDO supervision, conducting tests with the same professionalism and standards as an FAA ASI, but without enforcement or investigative authority.
- Role of the Flight Instructor: The section "Role of the Flight Instructor" establishes that flight instructors are the cornerstone of aviation safety, bearing full responsibility for training pilots in all necessary knowledge and skills for safe operation in the National Airspace System. A good instructor understands the learning process, communicates effectively, uses a syllabus for proper habit development, and serves as a role model for safety practices.
- Sources of Flight Training: The section "Sources of Flight Training" outlines the primary options for flight training in the United States, which include FAA-approved pilot schools (14 CFR part 141), FAA-approved training centers (14 CFR part 142), non-certificated flying schools, and independent flight instructors. Part 141 schools offer structured curricula that can lead to reduced flight time requirements for certification, while part 142 training centers specialize in flight simulation.
- Airman Certification Standards (ACS) and Practical Test Standards (PTS): The Airman Certification Standards (ACS) and Practical Test Standards (PTS) are testing documents used for FAA pilot certificate practical tests, outlining structured areas of operation, tasks, and standards that applicants must meet. These documents are not intended as a training syllabus but rather specify the standards for maneuvers and procedures on practical tests, guiding flight instructors in training applicants to acceptable levels.
- Safety Considerations: Safety Considerations involve basic flight safety practices and procedures that instructors should emphasize and both instructors and learners should adhere to from the very first dual instruction flight. These include collision avoidance, runway incursion avoidance, stall awareness, positive transfer of controls, and flight deck workload management.
- Collision Avoidance: The "Collision Avoidance" section emphasizes that pilots must maintain constant vigilance through the "see and avoid" concept, as most midair collisions occur in good VFR conditions near airports. This requires proper visual scanning techniques to compensate for human eye limitations and high closure rates, while being aware that scanning effectiveness can be degraded by various psychological and physiological conditions.
- Runway Incursion Avoidance: The "Runway Incursion Avoidance" section defines a runway incursion as any airport occurrence creating a collision hazard or loss of separation with an aircraft. Major contributing factors include communications, airport knowledge, and flight deck procedures, with vigilance, planning, clear communications, and enhanced situational awareness emphasized to reduce these incidents.
- Stall Awareness: Stall Awareness training, required by 14 CFR part 61, emphasizes that an excessive angle of attack (AOA) is the direct cause of every stall, regardless of airspeed or pitch attitude. Pilots must develop the skill to visualize the wing's AOA and understand influencing factors for flight safety.
- Use of Checklists: The "Use of Checklists" is foundational for pilot standardization and flight deck safety, serving as a memory aid to ensure critical items are not overlooked. Consistent and disciplined use of these checklists, which are used to confirm actions across various flight phases, is crucial in pilot training to establish good habit patterns.
- Continuing Education: The section on Continuing Education emphasizes that the ability to receive feedback and continue learning contributes to safety and success in many activities. This is exemplified by professional athletes who receive constant coaching and medical professionals who read journals and train to achieve better outcomes.
- FAA WINGS Program: The FAA WINGS Program offers continuous pilot education and relevant study materials, allowing pilots to exceed minimum training requirements and enhance proficiency and safety. It provides access to aviation technology and risk mitigation information, helps document skill development, and may offer discounts on flight insurance policies.
