## Main Concepts
- Gas Turbine Engine: The Gas Turbine Engine is an internal combustion engine where the phases of power, including induction, compression, combustion, expansion, and exhaust, occur simultaneously and continuously. It operates by compressing air, igniting a fuel/air mixture for continuous combustion, and using the expanding hot gases to rotate a turbine that drives the compressor, with ignition occurring during the starting cycle and continuing thereafter.
- Turboprop Engines: Turboprop engines combine the best characteristics of turbojet and propeller-driven reciprocating engines, offering advantages like light weight, reliability, and high power per unit of weight. They are most efficient at speeds between 250 and 400 mph and altitudes between 18,000 and 30,000 feet, with power measured in shaft horsepower (shp) and some jet thrust contributing to equivalent shaft horsepower (eshp).
- Turboprop Engine Types: The "Fixed-Shaft" is one type of turboprop engine, such as the Garrett TPE331, where compressed air mixes with atomized fuel in a combustor, and the expanding gases drive a turbine that converts energy to torque for the propeller via a reduction gear. In this turboprop engine type, power changes are made by increasing fuel flow and propeller blade angle to maintain a constant engine speed in flight.
- Split-Shaft/Free Turbine Engine: The Split-Shaft/Free Turbine Engine, such as the Pratt & Whitney PT-6, drives the propeller via a separate turbine and reduction gearing, distinct from the basic engine's turbine and compressor shaft. This design allows the propeller to be feathered while the engine runs and enables the pilot to select a desired propeller governing rpm independently of the basic engine rpm.
- Reverse Thrust and Beta Range Operations: Reverse Thrust and Beta Range Operations explain how propellers generate braking action by positioning blades to a negative pitch, pushing air away from the airplane. The beta range of operation, engaged by moving the power lever aft of flight idle, allows direct control of propeller blade angle from flat pitch to maximum reverse for ground operations.
- Turboprop Airplane Electrical Systems: Turboprop airplane electrical systems are typically 28-volt DC, receiving power from batteries and starter/generators that function as both engine starters and power generators. This DC power is distributed through various buses and can be converted to 28-volt 400-cycle AC by an inverter for specific avionic, lighting, and indicator synchronization functions.
- Operational Considerations: Operational Considerations for turboprop airplanes highlight key differences from piston engines, primarily concerning engine and systems operation. Pilots transitioning to turboprops must be aware of power output lag in split-shaft/free turbine engines, requiring anticipation of power changes, and the critical heat sensitivity of turbine engines, especially during startup, necessitating vigilant temperature monitoring.
- Training Considerations: Training Considerations for turboprop airplanes highlight the unique medium and high-altitude environment, necessitating thorough ground training on aspects like physiology, weather, and systems. Flight training should prepare pilots with comprehensive knowledge of airplane performance, emergency procedures, and operating limitations, along with high proficiency in all flight maneuvers.
- Ground Training: Ground Training covers the high-altitude flight environment. This includes airspace and Reduced Vertical Separation Minimum (RVSM) Operations, along with Title 14 Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 91, section 91.211 requirements for use.
- Supplemental Oxygen: The "Supplemental Oxygen" section outlines physiological training topics such as respiration, hypoxia, and the effects of prolonged oxygen use. It also covers high-altitude systems, including oxygen and oxygen equipment, pressurization, and emergency procedures for oxygen mask use or supply failure.
- Flight Training: Flight Training covers a comprehensive sequence of procedures, beginning with preflight briefing, planning, and inspection, which includes a functional test of the oxygen system. It progresses through engine start, takeoff, climb to high altitude cruise, and planned descents, while also covering emergencies like simulated rapid decompression, and concludes with shutdown procedures and postflight discussion.

It also explains turbopropeller electrical systems, operational considerations like starting and high temperatures, and provides training considerations with a sample syllabus for pilot proficiency.
