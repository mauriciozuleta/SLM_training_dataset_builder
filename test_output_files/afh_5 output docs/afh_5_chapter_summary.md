## Main Concepts
- Defining an Airplane Upset: The section "Defining an Airplane Upset" explains that the term "upset" was formally introduced in 2004 to describe an event where an airplane unintentionally exceeds normal flight parameters. These parameters include pitch attitudes greater than 25° nose up or 10° nose down, bank angles greater than 45°, or inappropriate airspeeds for the conditions.
- Upset Prevention and Recovery: Upset Prevention and Recovery training introduces pilots to unusual attitudes, defined as unintended or unexpected attitudes in instrument flight, and focuses on how to recognize and recover from them throughout various stages of pilot certification.
- Unusual Attitudes Versus Upsets: The section "Unusual Attitudes Versus Upsets" distinguishes between these two, noting that an upset includes stall events, overspeeds, or inappropriate speeds, and has defined parameters that an unusual attitude may not meet. Unlike unusual attitude training, upsets center on unintentional situations that can lead to a startle effect, with environmental, mechanical, human, and stall-related factors being top causes for upsets resulting in LOC-I accidents.
- Environmental Factors: Environmental Factors such as various types of turbulence, including clear air, mountain waves, wind shear, thunderstorms, microbursts, and wake turbulence, can cause upset and LOC-I. Additionally, icing significantly degrades airplane performance by disrupting airflow, increasing drag, and decreasing lift, potentially leading to a stall.
- Mechanical Factors: Mechanical Factors, such as asymmetrical flaps, malfunctioning flight controls, runaway trim, or autoflight system issues, can cause departures from normal flight. Pilots should maintain proficiency in manual flight and possess knowledge of systems and procedures to minimize the impact of these anomalies and prevent upsets.
- Human Factors VMC to IMC: Regarding Human Factors VMC to IMC, accident reports indicate that continued VFR flight into marginal VMC and IMC is a factor contributing to LOC-I. A loss of the natural horizon substantially increases the chances of encountering vertigo or spatial disorientation, which can lead to upset.
- Diversion of Attention: Diversion of Attention can lead to an upset if a pilot's focus is drawn from basic airplane control responsibilities, often due to in-flight anomalies or malfunctions. This can also occur from issues with automated systems, such as over-reliance or incomplete knowledge, or simply from efforts to set avionics or navigation equipment.
- Task Saturation: Task Saturation occurs when task requirements exceed pilot capabilities, reducing the margin of safety and potentially leading to an upset or Loss of Control-Inflight (LOC-I). This can happen in situations such as demanding upset recovery maneuvers, unpracticed partial panel flight due to equipment failure, or unnecessary low-altitude flying.
- Sensory Overload/Deprivation: The section on Sensory Overload/Deprivation explains that pilots can be overwhelmed by multiple simultaneous warnings during an upset, or distracted by missing expected warnings. It emphasizes that practice, experience, and knowledge, along with cross-checks, are crucial for pilots to differentiate critical information from distractions and identify missing or invalid cues.
- Spatial Disorientation: Spatial disorientation is a significant factor in many airplane upset accidents, often leading to fatalities, as all pilots are susceptible to false sensory illusions that conflict with actual attitude indications. Pilots may experience spatial disorientation as recognized (correctable), unrecognized (unaware of upset), or incapacitating (unable to recover).
- Surprise and Startle Response: The section "Surprise and Startle Response" defines surprise as an unexpected event affecting mental processes and startle as an uncontrollable, automatic physiological reflex to sudden, intense events, noting these responses have been underestimated in flight training. Scenario-based training incorporating realistic distractions and perceived risk can help prepare pilots to mitigate these psychological and physiological reactions during actual upset events.
- Upset Prevention and Recovery Training (UPRT): Upset Prevention and Recovery Training (UPRT) addresses the risk of unexpected upsets, which can lead to instinctive and hazardous pilot reactions, potentially escalating into fatal LOC-I accidents. UPRT focuses on educating pilots to prevent upsets through awareness and early recognition, and to recover from developing or fully-developed upsets by returning the airplane to a desired state with appropriate control inputs.
- UPRT Training Core Concepts: UPRT Training Core Concepts focus on equipping pilots to promptly recognize and correct impending airplane upsets, which are time-critical events. This training aims to reduce surprise and confusion by exposing pilots to unusual attitudes in a structured, non-threatening environment, reinforcing perishable skills in both visual and simulated instrument conditions to improve recognition and recovery.
- Academic Material (Knowledge and Risk Management): Academic Material (Knowledge and Risk Management) establishes the foundation for situational awareness, insight, knowledge, and skills, progressing from general to specific concepts. This academic preparation is crucial for mitigating the LOC-I threat, with long-term retention best achieved when correlated with practical experience, and it should also address prevention concepts like aeronautical-decision making, risk management, and proportional counter response.
- Prevention Through ADM and Risk Management: Prevention Through ADM and Risk Management involves effective analysis, awareness, resource management, and interrupting the error chain to avert threatening flight conditions. Pilots should evaluate flight circumstances for scenarios requiring higher risk management, utilizing crew resource management (CRM) or single-pilot resource management (SRM) to enhance safety and situational awareness.
- Prevention Through Proportional Counter-Response: Prevention Through Proportional Counter-Response involves the timely manipulation of flight controls and thrust to manage unintended flight attitude or envelope excursions, aiming to recognize and avoid a fully-developed upset. This typically occurs in seconds or fractions of seconds, but carries a high risk of panic and overreaction due to the sudden nature of developing upsets.
- Recovery: The "Recovery" section highlights that academic knowledge, procedures, and techniques form the foundation for safe upset recovery, which is then translated into structured practice using airplane and FSTD-based training. Pilots can utilize flight instruments for recognition and recovery, following AFM/POH procedures for nose-high and nose-low attitudes, while being mindful of common errors like incorrect assessment or inappropriate airspeed management.
- Roles of FSTDs and Airplanes in UPRT: This section discusses the Roles of FSTDs and Airplanes in UPRT, noting that training devices have varying capabilities. Only higher fidelity FSTDs (Level C and D FFS) are a satisfactory substitute for developing UPRT skills in an actual aircraft; otherwise, initial skill development should occur in a suitable airplane.
- Airplane-Based UPRT: Airplane-Based UPRT offers a more realistic and indelible learning experience than simulators, improving a pilot’s ability to overcome fear during upset events. However, its effectiveness is limited by approved aircraft maneuvers and the instructor's UPRT capabilities, necessitating specialized instructors to prevent negative learning.
- All-Attitude/All-Envelope Flight Training Methods: All-Attitude/All-Envelope Flight Training Methods, as part of sound UPRT, encompass operation in a wide range of flight attitudes and the airplane’s limit flight envelope to prepare pilots for unexpected upsets. These non-airplane-specific skills focus on the avoidance of, and safe recovery from, upsets, providing lasting lessons for pilots.
- FSTD–based UPRT: FSTD–based UPRT can be effective in high fidelity devices (Level C and D FFS), though instructors and pilots must be mindful of technical and physiological boundaries. This training is a current, FAA-approved requirement for pilots seeking a multiengine airplane ATP certificate.
- Coordinated Flight: Coordinated flight occurs when the pilot proactively corrects for yaw effects associated with power, aileron inputs, turning, and airplane rigging. An airplane is in coordinated flight when its nose is yawed directly into the relative wind and the ball is centered in the slip/skid indicator.
- Angle of Attack: The Angle of Attack (AOA) is defined as the angle between the wing's chord and the relative wind, and exceeding a wing's critical AOA leads to a stall due to airflow separation, which reduces lift and increases drag. Pilots must understand that a stall is caused by exceeding the critical AOA, not insufficient airspeed, and that stall speed is not constant but varies with factors like load factor, gross weight, and flap setting.
- Slow Flight: Slow Flight training aims to develop a pilot's ability to maneuver an airplane at low airspeeds and high angles of attack, familiarizing them with the associated feel, sound, visual cues, and degraded control response. Pilots learn to maintain controlled flight without activating a stall warning, typically 5 to 10 knots above the 1G stall speed, in various flight configurations.
- Performing the Slow Flight Maneuver: Performing the Slow Flight Maneuver involves smoothly slowing the airplane from cruising to approach speeds while maintaining altitude and heading, increasing the angle of attack, and setting required power and trim. Pilots initiate the maneuver by reducing thrust and adjusting pitch, then extend landing gear and flaps, adding power and trim as speed decreases to maintain altitude, typically performing it in the landing configuration at a safe altitude above ground.
- Maneuvering in Slow Flight: When maneuvering in slow flight, pilots must maintain awareness, cross-check instruments, and practice turns, climbs, and descents, while being mindful of control characteristics and avoiding abrupt movements to prevent stalls. Exiting slow flight involves adding power, reducing AOA, cleaning up the airplane, and anticipating AOA changes to maintain control.
- Stalls: Stalls are an aerodynamic condition where smooth airflow over an airplane’s wings is disrupted, resulting in a loss of lift when the angle of attack (AOA) exceeds the wing’s critical AOA. This condition can occur at any airspeed, attitude, or power setting, and is practiced as either an impending stall, indicated by warnings, or a full stall, where the critical AOA is exceeded, leading to uncommanded pitch or roll.
- Stall Recognition: Stall Recognition involves a pilot's ability to identify flight conditions conducive to stalls and impending stalls through various cues. These cues include stall warning devices, inherent aerodynamic qualities like pre-stall buffeting, and sensory inputs such as changes in control feel, air sound, and kinesthetic sensations.
- Angle of Attack Indicators: Angle of Attack (AOA) indicators enhance pilot situational awareness by visually representing the current AOA and its proximity to the critical AOA, thus improving stall margin awareness and helping prevent loss of control in flight. These devices measure several parameters to show the airplane's energy management state, but proper installation and training are essential for their effective use.
- Stall Characteristics: Stall Characteristics differ across airplane designs, influenced by factors such as geometry, center of gravity, and wing design, requiring pilots to know their specific aircraft's characteristics and recovery procedures. Most training airplanes are designed for progressive stalling from the wing roots outward, but pilots must reduce the angle of attack before attempting to roll the airplane to avoid exacerbating the stalled condition.
- Fundamentals of Stall Recovery: The "Fundamentals of Stall Recovery" section emphasizes that the most important action for an impending or full stall is to reduce the Angle of Attack (AOA). It provides a generic stall recovery procedure for light general aviation aircraft, which includes disconnecting autopilot, pitching nose-down to reduce AOA, rolling wings level, and adding thrust/power, while always adhering to aircraft-specific manufacturer's recommended procedures.
- Stall Training: Stall Training, encompassing both power-on and power-off stalls, is crucial for pilots to understand and simulate conditions that could lead to inadvertent stalls during normal flight maneuvers, such as after takeoff, during climbing turns, or on approach to landing. This practice helps pilots develop awareness of potential stall scenarios and ensures they practice at a safe altitude for recovery.
- Approaches to Stalls (Impending Stalls), Power-On or Power-Off: The section "Approaches to Stalls (Impending Stalls), Power-On or Power-Off" describes an impending stall as an approach to, but not exceeding, the critical AOA, with the purpose of teaching pilots to immediately regain control upon recognizing stall indications. Recovery involves promptly reducing AOA at the first sign of a stall warning or buffeting, holding a nose-down input, leveling the wings, maintaining coordinated flight, and applying power to return to the desired flightpath.
- Full Stalls, Power-Off: The section "Full Stalls, Power-Off" details the practice of power-off stalls, typically performed under normal landing approach conditions with the aircraft in landing configuration. Pilots should decelerate to approach speed, set power to idle, and then smoothly raise the nose to induce the stall while maintaining directional control. Recovery involves reducing the angle of attack, applying nose-down input, leveling the wings, and applying power as needed.
- Full Stalls, Power-On: This section on Full Stalls, Power-On, details practicing these stalls from straight climbs and climbing turns to recognize potential accidental stalls during takeoff, go-around, or climb. Pilots set the airplane in takeoff/climb configuration, slow to lift-off speed, apply takeoff/climb power, and smoothly raise the nose to increase AOA until a full stall occurs, then recover by immediately reducing AOA and applying nose-down control.
- Secondary Stall: A secondary stall occurs after recovery from a preceding stall, often when a pilot attempts an abrupt recovery that exceeds the critical angle of attack again or fails to sufficiently reduce the angle of attack. To recover from a secondary stall, the pilot should apply nose-down elevator pressure, level the wings with ailerons, coordinate with rudder, and adjust power as needed.
- Accelerated Stalls: Accelerated stalls occur when an airplane is subjected to an acceleration greater than +1G, causing it to stall at a higher indicated airspeed than its +1G stall speed. Training for accelerated stalls aims to help pilots understand these characteristics and develop instinctive recovery skills, though they should never be practiced with wing flaps extended due to lower design G-load limitations.
- Cross-Control Stall: The Cross-Control Stall demonstration highlights the effects of uncoordinated flight on stall behavior and the importance of maintaining coordinated flight during turns. This stall occurs when the critical angle of attack is exceeded with opposing aileron and rudder pressures, often during a poorly executed base-to-final approach turn, and can lead to a rapid entry into a spin.
- Elevator Trim Stall: The elevator trim stall demonstration illustrates what can happen when a pilot applies full power for a go-around without maintaining positive control of the airplane. It highlights the importance of smooth power applications, overcoming strong trim forces, and using proper trim techniques to prevent a stall during such maneuvers.
- Spin Awareness: Spin Awareness defines a spin as an aggravated stall condition where the airplane follows a downward corkscrew path, resulting from mishandling yaw control during a stall. A spin occurs when at least one wing exceeds the critical angle of attack with a sideslip or yaw, and maintaining directional control by preventing yaw before stall recovery is key to averting it.
- Spin Procedures: The "Spin Procedures" section emphasizes that the first rule for spin demonstrations is ensuring the airplane is approved, followed by a careful review of the AFM/POH for limitations, weight and balance, and recommended entry/recovery procedures. Essential safety measures also include a thorough preflight inspection, clearing the flight area, and beginning spins at an altitude high enough to complete recovery at or above 1,500 feet AGL.
- Entry Phase: The Entry Phase details how a pilot initiates a spin, either intentionally or accidentally, by providing the necessary elements. This procedure involves reducing power to idle, raising the nose to a pitch attitude that ensures a stall, and then applying full rudder in the desired spin direction along with full back elevator as the airplane approaches a stall.
- Incipient Phase: The incipient phase occurs from the time an airplane stalls and starts rotating until the spin has fully developed, typically taking two to four turns as aerodynamic and inertial forces are not yet balanced. During this phase, the turn indicator shows the spin direction, and pilots should initiate recovery by applying full rudder opposite the rotation prior to completing 360° of rotation, a common maneuver in initial spin training.
- Developed Phase: The Developed Phase occurs when an airplane's angular rotation rate, airspeed, and vertical speed are stabilized in a nearly vertical flightpath, with aerodynamic and inertial forces in balance, signifying the spin is in equilibrium. It is important to note that some training airplanes may not enter this phase, instead transitioning into an accelerating spiral dive.
- Recovery Phase: The Recovery Phase occurs when rotation ceases and the angle of attack decreases below critical. To recover from a spin, pilots must apply control inputs to disrupt spin equilibrium by stopping rotation and unstalling the wing, following manufacturer's procedures or a six-step process including reducing power, neutralizing ailerons, applying full opposite rudder, and forward elevator.
- Intentional Spins: Intentional spins are not authorized by CFRs or suggested by this handbook unless the aircraft manufacturer specifically approves the aircraft for them. Approval for intentional spins can be determined from official sources such as Type Certificate Data Sheets, the limitation section of the FAA-approved AFM/POH, or placards in the airplane.
- MANEUVERS INCLUDING SPINS APPROVED”): In airplanes placarded against spins, as discussed in 'MANEUVERS INCLUDING SPINS APPROVED', there is no assurance of recovery from a fully developed spin. This is because many such airplanes were only certified to recover from a one-turn or three-second spin, not a fully developed one, meaning pilots should assume the airplane could become uncontrollable in a spin.
- Weight and Balance Requirement Related to Spins: The section on Weight and Balance Requirement Related to Spins highlights that adherence to weight and balance requirements is crucial for safe spin performance and recovery, as even minor changes can significantly alter an airplane's spin characteristics and recovery. An airplane approved for spins in one category might not recover if loaded according to another category's limits, even if within CG, due to altered spin entry and recovery characteristics.
- Spiral Dive: A spiral dive is a nose-low upset characterized by a rapidly increasing airspeed and G-load during a descending turn, often initiated by pilots relying on kinesthetic sensations during inadvertent IMC encounters. To recover from a spiral dive, pilots should immediately reduce power to idle, apply forward-elevator pressure to unload the G-load, and then roll to wings level using coordinated aileron and rudder inputs.

Effective learning involves understanding the context of UPRT procedures, starting with broad applicability before narrowing to type-specific requirements.
