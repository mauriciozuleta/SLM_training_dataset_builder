## Main Concepts
- Emergency Landings: This section on Emergency Landings details techniques for small fixed-wing airplanes, particularly for adverse terrain conditions where practical training is not feasible. The objective is to equip pilots with the knowledge to utilize the airplane structure for self-protection, making almost any terrain suitable for a survivable crash landing.
- Types of Emergency Landings: This section defines the types of emergency landings: forced landing, precautionary landing, and ditching. A forced landing is an immediate landing due to inability to continue flight, a precautionary landing is a premeditated landing when further flight is inadvisable, and ditching is any emergency landing on water.
- Psychological Hazards: Psychological Hazards can interfere with a pilot's ability to act promptly and properly during an emergency, stemming from factors such as reluctance to accept the situation, undue concern about getting hurt, and the desire to save the airplane, which can lead to errors and risky decisions.
- Basic Safety Concepts: The section on Basic Safety Concepts emphasizes that avoiding crash injuries in an emergency landing relies on keeping the vital cabin area intact by using dispensable airplane structure to absorb impact and preventing forceful bodily contact with interior structure through proper restraint. Survivability is also influenced by the use of external energy-absorbing mediums and the critical role of groundspeed, as doubling it quadruples destructive energy.
- Attitude and Sink Rate Control: Attitude and Sink Rate Control is critical during emergency landings, as loss of initiative can lead to errors like excessive nose-low pitch or steep bank angles, increasing risks. High sink rates, especially over 500 fpm, can cause injury or structural damage on hard surfaces, and digging in on soft terrain.
- Terrain Selection: Terrain selection for emergency landings is governed by the preflight route, height above ground, and excess airspeed. Pilots should prioritize energy-absorbing terrain if beyond gliding distance, focusing on general areas from altitude, and generally avoid changing their mind more than once, as a well-executed crash landing in poor terrain can be less hazardous than an uncontrolled touchdown on an established field.
- Airplane Configuration: The "Airplane Configuration" section provides guidance on managing aircraft components during emergency landings, recommending cautious flap use and weighing the benefits and risks of extended landing gear, with manufacturer recommendations taking precedence. It also advises deactivating the electrical system and turning off the engine just before touchdown to mitigate fire hazards, while emphasizing that positive airplane control during the final approach has priority over all other considerations.
- Approach: The "Approach" section outlines that planning an emergency approach should be governed by wind direction and velocity, field dimensions and slope, and obstacles in the final approach path. Pilots should aim for a combination that permits a margin for error, prioritizing an unobstructed approach path to minimize the hazard of striking obstacles at flying speed.
- Confined Areas: When landing in confined areas, pilots should prioritize reaching the ground safely, even if it means forcing touchdown, rather than stalling, and consider options like ground-looping or retracting landing gear. Additionally, when considering rivers or roads, pilots must ensure clear access to the touchdown surface and be vigilant for hidden obstacles like power lines.
- Trees (Forest): The "Trees (Forest)" section outlines guidelines for a survivable tree landing, recommending a normal landing configuration with full flaps and gear down, heading into the wind at minimum indicated airspeed in a nose-high attitude. It advises symmetrical initial contact involving the underside of the fuselage and both wings for cushioning, while avoiding direct contact with heavy tree trunks and preferring low, dense trees.
- Water (Ditching) and Snow: The section on Water (Ditching) and Snow details that a well-executed water landing at minimum speed can provide several minutes of flotation, but pilots must be aware of depth perception loss and "drag in" the airplane, using intermediate flaps on low-wing aircraft and keeping gear up. A landing in snow should be executed similarly to a ditching, with the same configuration and regard for loss of depth perception (whiteout).
- Engine Failure After Takeoff (Single-Engine): When an engine failure occurs after takeoff in a single-engine airplane, the pilot must immediately lower the nose to establish a glide attitude. The pilot then faces the critical decision of continuing straight ahead, which is generally safer, or attempting a risky turn back to the airport, which requires specific training and consideration of altitude loss to avoid potential disaster.
- Emergency Descents: An emergency descent is a maneuver for rapidly descending to a lower altitude or the ground for an emergency landing, necessitated by situations like an uncontrollable fire or sudden loss of cabin pressurization. The objective is to descend as quickly as possible without exceeding structural limitations, typically by reducing power to idle, placing the propeller in low pitch, and extending landing gear and flaps as recommended to maximize drag.
- In-Flight Fire: In-Flight Fire demands immediate and decisive action, requiring pilots to be familiar with specific procedures outlined in the aircraft's AFM/POH. For the purposes of this handbook, in-flight fires are classified as in-flight engine fires, electrical fires, and cabin fires.
- Engine Fire: An in-flight Engine Fire is usually caused by flammable substances contacting hot surfaces due to mechanical failures or maintenance errors, indicated by smoke, flames, or cowling discoloration. Initial pilot actions involve shutting off the fuel supply and, for oil-fed fires, stopping propeller rotation, while carefully considering deactivating the electrical system.
- Electrical Fires: Electrical fires are typically indicated by the distinct odor of burning insulation, prompting pilots to identify the faulty circuit by checking breakers, instruments, avionics, and lights. If the faulty circuit cannot be readily detected and isolated, and flight conditions permit, the battery master and alternator/generator switches should be turned off, with landing as soon as possible being the most prudent course of action.
- Cabin Fire: Cabin fires generally result from careless smoking, electrical, or heating system malfunctions, presenting the pilot with immediate demands to attack the fire and land the airplane safely and quickly. Control involves identifying and shutting down the faulty system, managing smoke removal through vents or depressurization, and initiating an immediate descent with oxygen masks if smoke is severe.
- Flight Control Malfunction/Failure: Total Flap Failure necessitates a no-flap approach and landing, which requires substantially more runway and a nose-high attitude that can hinder visibility and make altitude loss difficult. Pilots must manage increased float during roundout and avoid excessive flaring to prevent a tail strike.
- Asymmetric (Split) Flap: An asymmetric "split" flap situation occurs when one flap deploys or retracts while the other remains in position, causing a pronounced roll toward the wing with the least flap deflection, which requires opposite aileron and substantial opposite rudder. Pilots should fly the approach at a higher than normal airspeed, avoid landing with a crosswind from the side of the deployed flap, and fly the airplane onto the runway to prevent an asymmetric stall.
- Loss of Elevator Control: Loss of Elevator Control typically results in a partial loss of pitch control, as elevators are often managed by separate “up” and “down” cables. Pilots can retain pitch control by applying specific control yoke pressures and utilizing trim mechanisms, with techniques varying based on whether the “up” or “down” elevator cable has failed.
- Landing Gear Malfunction: In a Landing Gear Malfunction, if a gear-up landing is inevitable, the pilot should select an airport with crash and rescue facilities, consider runway surface options, and burn off excess fuel to reduce fire potential. If only one gear fails, the pilot has options to land on available gear or with all gear retracted, each requiring specific techniques to manage directional control and minimize damage.
- System Malfunctions: The "System Malfunctions" section highlights the criticality of electrical system failures, often stemming from generator or alternator issues, which can deprive pilots of essential systems. When the primary generating source fails, the battery provides limited power, necessitating immediate shedding of non-essential loads and planning to land, as high-draw items like landing gear can rapidly deplete the battery.
- Pitot-Static System: The pitot-static system provides pressure for the airspeed indicator, vertical speed indicator, and altimeter, and its components are subject to blockage by ice or dirt. Blockage, especially partial static system blockage, can cause insidious instrument malfunctions leading to inaccurate readings, requiring pilots to confirm and use an alternate static source if suspected.
- Abnormal Engine Instrument Indication: The section on Abnormal Engine Instrument Indication states that the AFM/POH provides specific information to follow for any abnormal engine instrument indications. Additionally, a generic table offers information on common in-flight abnormal engine instrument indications, their possible causes, and corrective actions.
- Door Opening In-Flight: For a door opening in-flight, the primary concern is the pilot's reaction and potential distraction, not the door itself compromising flight safety. Pilots should concentrate on flying the airplane, avoid panic, and land normally, as accidents are typically caused by loss of control due to distraction rather than the open door.
- Inadvertent VFR Flight Into IMC: This section on Inadvertent VFR Flight Into IMC provides guidance on practical emergency measures for VFR pilots encountering instrument meteorological conditions (IMC) to maintain airplane control for a limited time. The main goal is to keep the airplane under adequate control until suitable visual references are regained, starting with recognition, maintaining control, and obtaining assistance.
- Recognition: The "Recognition" section explains that a VFR pilot is considered to be in IMC if unable to maintain airplane attitude control by reference to the natural horizon or navigate by visual landmarks, regardless of actual weather. These situations are genuine emergencies, as untrained VFR pilots cannot control an airplane solely by instruments for long and risk vertigo, spatial disorientation, and inevitable control loss.
- Maintaining Airplane Control: Maintaining Airplane Control necessitates that pilots trust flight instruments exclusively, as attempts to visually confirm instrument data or rely on natural senses can lead to spatial disorientation and control loss. Pilots must avoid panic, relax, and focus on keeping the wings level, believing what the flight instruments indicate about the airplane's attitude.
- Attitude Control: Attitude Control in an emergency emphasizes trimming the airplane for hands-off level flight at cruise airspeed, resisting over-control, and making smooth, small attitude changes. The primary instrument for attitude control is the attitude indicator, and pilots should use fingertip pressure to maintain straight flight.
- Turns: Turns are potentially dangerous for untrained instrument pilots due to over-control leading to steep banks or a "graveyard spiral," and an inability to cope with instability. To mitigate these risks, pilots should use the smallest practical bank angle, ideally no more than 10°, and may repeat short turns to achieve large heading changes, preventing progressive overbanking.
- Climbs: When performing climbs, pilots should raise the miniature airplane on the attitude indicator no more than one bar width and apply power, accepting the resulting speed to minimize disturbance to the airplane's equilibrium. Pilots must anticipate and compensate for the airplane's tendency to bank and turn left due to torque and P-factor, increasing power incrementally as needed, and then decrease pitch and power to resume level flight.
- Descents: Descents are the opposite of climbs, where a power reduction causes the airplane to pitch down to maintain trimmed airspeed, resulting in a descent proportionate to the thrust removed. Power reductions should be made in increments of 100 rpm or 1 inch of manifold pressure, ensuring the rate of descent does not exceed 500 fpm while maintaining level wings and controlled pitch attitude.
- Combined Maneuvers: Combined maneuvers, such as climbing or descending turns, should be avoided by untrained instrument pilots due to increased risk of control loss and compounded problems. Pilots should prioritize maintaining airplane control and can request ATC to slow down if prompted to rush into such maneuvers.
- Transition to Visual Flight: Transition to Visual Flight is a difficult task, especially for untrained instrument pilots, due to challenges in acclimatization and geographical orientation. Pilots should allow sufficient time to become acclimated and oriented before attempting an approach and landing, particularly when conditions are not ideal.
- Emergency Response Systems: Emergency Response Systems in airplanes provide alternatives in certain emergency situations. These systems can include ballistic parachute systems, which allow for a slow descent and occupant survival, and Emergency Autoland (EAL) systems, which can take over aircraft control for a safe outcome.
- Ballistic Parachutes: Ballistic parachutes are designed to prevent injuries and save lives during catastrophic events, even though their deployment results in the loss of the airframe. Pilots must understand and follow procedures for arming, disarming, and deploying these systems under specific conditions, such as loss of control or pilot incapacitation, and brief passengers on safe deployment and post-landing evacuation procedures.
- Autoland: The Autoland (EAL) system automatically stabilizes an aircraft, initiates an emergency descent, and performs an automated landing if pilot responsiveness is absent, or it can be manually activated. This system transmits emergency broadcasts, sets the transponder to 7700, and selects a suitable airfield based on factors like weather and runway length, though it currently cannot detect other aircraft or react to ATC instructions.

It also discussed challenges related to pitot-static system failures in EFIS aircraft, including recognition difficulties, standardization issues, and the need for enhanced training for advanced electronic avionics.
