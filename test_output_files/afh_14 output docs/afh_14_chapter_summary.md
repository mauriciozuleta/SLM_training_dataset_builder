## Main Concepts
- Landing Gear: The Landing Gear section details how tailwheel airplanes' main landing gear is positioned ahead of the center of gravity, resulting in a nose-high attitude and unique handling characteristics. Directional control during taxiing primarily relies on rudder pedals, which may steer the tailwheel mechanically or through airflow, while brakes are used for slowing and turning, requiring smooth application.
- Instability: Tailwheel aircraft exhibit inherent instability on the ground due to the relative placement of the main gear and the center of gravity, causing turns to tighten without further steering inputs. Pilots transitioning to these aircraft must learn to use opposite rudder to stop turns and should always taxi and make turns at slow speeds to manage this instability.
- Angle of Attack: Regarding "Angle of Attack," tailwheel aircraft generate lift on the ground with a relative headwind, with the amount of lift depending on wind speed. This phenomenon, where wings and ailerons aid liftoff even at slow taxi speeds, requires careful management during takeoff and landing rolls and is a significant contrast for transitioning nose-wheel pilots.
- Taxiing: Taxiing a tailwheel airplane primarily uses a steerable tailwheel linked to the rudder for directional control, with pilots advised to use rudder for normal turns and test brakes immediately. Due to the airplane's pitch attitude, specific control inputs, such as holding ailerons into the wind and the elevator full back, are crucial for managing lift and improving steering response, especially in varying wind conditions.
- Weathervaning: Weathervaning describes the exaggerated tendency of tailwheel airplanes to turn into the wind when operated on the ground in crosswinds. This effect is most pronounced during taxiing with a direct crosswind, making directional control difficult and sometimes requiring brake use when tailwheel steering is insufficient.
- Visibility: Visibility in tailwheel airplanes is restricted on the ground due to the nose-high attitude and engine cowling, making objects directly ahead difficult to see. Pilots should compensate by starting taxi movements with a small turn and then alternately turning the nose (zigzag or S-turns) slowly and cautiously.
- Directional Control: Regarding directional control, transitioning pilots should understand that tailwheel aircraft designs often require vigorous rudder inputs, not limited ones, to maintain or retain control. It is important to note that damage results from rudder inputs held for too long, rather than from using too much rudder.
- Normal Takeoff Roll: The normal takeoff roll in tailwheel airplanes begins with aligning the aircraft, centering the tailwheel, and smoothly advancing the throttle to takeoff power while avoiding brakes. Pilots must then smoothly lift the tail with positive elevator, managing left yaw from gyroscopic precession, and maintaining directional control with prompt rudder corrections as speed increases.
- Liftoff: Liftoff occurs when the appropriate pitch attitude, angle of attack, and airspeed combine to produce the necessary lift, ideally requiring minimal pitch adjustments post-liftoff to attain the desired climb speed. Pilots should maintain straight flight and proper pitch attitude, and in strong, gusty winds, it is advisable to add an extra margin of speed before liftoff to ensure positive control and prevent stalls.
- Crosswind Takeoff: For a Crosswind Takeoff, pilots must establish and maintain proper crosswind corrections prior to liftoff, using aileron deflection into the wind and rudder to prevent weathervaning. This involves maintaining a positive angle of attack, making the takeoff roll on the upwind main wheel, and adjusting controls as the airplane accelerates until liftoff.
- Short-Field Takeoff: Short-field takeoffs largely mirror normal takeoffs, with key differences in flap settings and initial climb speed, and require pitch adjustment after liftoff for obstacle clearance. Pilots should note that some manufacturers recommend a three-point attitude liftoff for short-field takeoffs and always follow the airplane manufacturer's recommended procedures.
- Soft-Field Takeoff: The Soft-Field Takeoff procedure involves lowering wing flaps (if recommended) to provide lift and transfer weight, while keeping the airplane in continuous motion on soft surfaces to prevent bogging down. During the takeoff roll, maintain a nose-high attitude to minimize drag from surface irregularities and avoid nosing over, then accelerate to climb speed in ground effect once airborne.
- Landing: The "Landing" section highlights the differences in touchdown and deceleration between nose-wheel and tailwheel airplanes. Nose-wheel aircraft reduce pitch and angle of attack (AOA) to transfer weight to tires, whereas tailwheel airplanes continue to "fly" in a three-point attitude after touchdown, requiring extended attention to heading, roll, and pitch.
- Touchdown: The "Touchdown" section emphasizes that tailwheel airplanes require precise touchdown with the longitudinal axis parallel to the direction of movement to prevent side loads and directional instability. It details two primary techniques: the three-point landing, where the main gear and tailwheel touch simultaneously, and the wheel landing, where the main gear touch first while the tail remains off the runway.
- Three-Point Landing: The Three-Point Landing technique aims to touch the tailwheel and main wheels simultaneously by achieving a steep fuselage angle after rounding out. Potential errors include the main wheels touching early, causing a “skip,” or the tail touching first if held off too long, with a crucial after-landing elevator input needed to press the tailwheel for directional control.
- Wheel Landing: A wheel landing is a technique used in certain wind conditions to retain control authority, where the airplane touches down on its main wheels at a higher airspeed and in a flatter pitch attitude. After a soft touchdown and slight relaxation of back elevator, the tail is allowed to drop, and a bounced wheel landing attempt should prompt a go-around or conversion to a three-point landing.
- Crosswinds: For crosswinds, it is highly desirable to eliminate crab and drift at touchdown, with a sideslip or wing-low touchdown being the best approach. Many tailwheel pilots prefer completing a wheel landing in crosswinds due to the higher initial touchdown speed, which makes flight controls more effective.
- After-Landing Roll: The "After-Landing Roll" section emphasizes that the landing process is not complete until the airplane reaches taxi speed or stops, requiring pilots to maintain directional control by holding the elevator back firmly to prevent swerving or ground loops. Pilots should apply brakes carefully, maintaining rudder pressure, to control direction and speed while using ailerons to counter any rising wing.
- Crosswind After-Landing Roll: During the crosswind after-landing roll, pilots must maintain directional control using rudder and tailwheel steering while applying aileron to keep the upwind wing from rising. As the airplane decelerates, more aileron is required due to decreased effectiveness and increased crosswind lift, necessitating the aileron control be held fully toward the wind when coming to a stop.
- Short-Field Landing: For a Short-Field Landing, the airplane should be firmly held in a three-point attitude upon touchdown, which provides aerodynamic braking. Immediately after touchdown and closing the throttle, brakes should be applied evenly and firmly to minimize the after-landing roll and stop within the shortest possible distance consistent with safety.
- Soft-Field Landing: For a Soft-Field Landing, the tailwheel should touch down simultaneously with or just before the main wheels, and firm back-elevator pressure must be maintained throughout the landing roll to minimize nosing over and provide aerodynamic braking. Brakes are not needed as the soft surface provides sufficient speed reduction, and pilots may need to increase power to prevent the airplane from becoming stuck.
- Ground Loop: A ground loop is an uncontrolled turn during ground operations, such as taxi or landing roll, which begins with a swerve caused by factors like side-load on landing or overcorrection. Due to the inherent instability of tailwheel designs, pilots must use firm rudder input and differential braking to counteract these turns, while promptly removing corrections to avoid an opposite departure.
